Thinking of Buying Backlinks? What Google's Rules Say, and What Works for a Small Business

Cheap backlink packages promise better rankings, but most of what they sell is on Google's list of link spam. Here is what the rules say and how to earn links safely.

Editorial Desk Published 15 Nov 2026, 7:26 pm IST 6 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

If you run a website, you have probably seen the offers. Five hundred backlinks for a few dollars. A hundred “high authority” blog comments. Fifty forum posts with your link in them. Each promises to lift your site up Google’s results.

Backlinks, meaning links from other websites to yours, do matter to search engines. That is exactly why a market has grown up to sell them. The difficulty is that most of what is sold in these packages appears, almost item by item, on Google’s own published list of practices it treats as spam.

Here is what the rules say, what the risk is, and what a small business can do that works.

Key points

  • Google defines link spam as creating links “primarily for the purpose of manipulating search rankings”.
  • Buying links, automated link-building, forum and comment links, and low-quality directory listings are all named as examples.
  • Since 2022 Google has mostly cancelled out such links, so the money buys nothing. In serious cases a site can be demoted or removed.
  • Paid links are allowed when they are labelled as paid.
  • Links earned through useful content, real relationships and local listings are slower but carry no such risk.

What a backlink is, and why it counts

When one website links to another, a search engine reads it as a kind of recommendation. A page that many trusted sites link to is more likely to be useful, so it tends to rank better. That idea was at the heart of Google’s original design and still plays a part.

The key word is trusted. A link from a respected newspaper, a university or a well-known site in your industry means something because nobody could simply buy it. A link that anyone can create in thirty seconds means nothing, and search engines have become good at telling the two apart.

What Google’s rules actually say

Google publishes its spam policies, and the section on links is specific. It lists these among its examples of link spam:

  • Buying or selling links for ranking purposes. This covers paying money for links or for posts containing links, exchanging goods or services for links, and sending someone a product in return for a link.
  • Using automated programs or services to create links to your site.
  • Forum comments with optimised links in the post or signature.
  • Low-quality directory or bookmark site links.
  • Excessive link exchanges, the “link to me and I’ll link to you” arrangement.
  • Paid articles, guest posts or press releases that carry links designed to pass ranking credit.
  • Links placed widely in the footers or templates of various sites.

Set that list beside a typical menu of cheap link packages and the overlap is close to complete.

What the package offers Where it stands under Google’s policy
“500 backlinks” delivered in days Automated link creation, named as spam
“Dofollow blog comment” links Comment links with optimised anchors, named as spam
Forum post or profile links Forum links, named as spam
“100 directory submissions” Low-quality directory links, named as spam
Paid guest posts with keyword links Paid posts passing ranking credit, named as spam

What happens if you buy them anyway

Two outcomes are possible, and neither is good value.

Most likely, nothing. In December 2022 Google announced that its spam-detection system, SpamBrain, could identify both sites that buy links and sites used to pass them on. Its approach is to neutralise such links, so that they simply stop counting. Google warned at the time that rankings may change as “any credit passed by these unnatural links are lost”. In plain terms: you pay, and the links are ignored.

Less likely, a penalty. Google says sites that break its spam policies “may rank lower or not appear in results at all”. A human reviewer can also issue what is called a manual action, which shows up in the site owner’s Search Console account and must be fixed before the site recovers.

There is a third cost that sellers rarely mention. Your website’s address ends up on hundreds of pages you did not choose and cannot easily remove.

Paid links are not banned. Undisclosed ones are

This is the part people most often misunderstand. Google accepts that paying for links is “a normal part of the economy of the web for advertising and sponsorship purposes”.

The rule is that a paid link must be labelled so that it does not pass ranking credit. In a web page’s code this is done with a small tag, rel="sponsored", on the link. Google’s guidance on outbound links describes three such labels: sponsored for paid placements, ugc for links in user comments and forum posts, and nofollow as a general-purpose option.

So advertising on another site, sponsoring an event or paying for a listing are all fine. Paying for a link that is dressed up as an independent recommendation is what breaks the rules.

What works instead

Good links are a by-product of being worth linking to. For a small business, that usually means some combination of the following.

  1. Publish something others need to cite. Original numbers travel furthest: a price survey of your local market, a simple calculator, a clear guide to a rule your customers find confusing.
  2. Claim your local listings. A complete Google Business Profile, and accurate entries in the directories your customers actually use, bring both links and customers. A relevant industry association or chamber of commerce listing is worth more than a hundred generic ones.
  3. Be useful to journalists. Reporters and trade publications need expert comment. A founder who answers quickly and plainly gets quoted, and quotes usually come with a link.
  4. Use the relationships you already have. Suppliers, customers, partners and event organisers often have pages where a link to you belongs naturally.
  5. Fix your own site first. Clear pages that load fast and answer the searcher’s question do more for rankings than most links. Internal links between your own pages cost nothing.

None of this is quick. Results typically take months. They also last, because nobody can take them away with an algorithm update.

How to judge a link-building service

Reputable agencies exist, and they mostly do the slow work above on your behalf. A few questions separate them from the rest.

  • Do they promise a number of links? Real outreach cannot guarantee a count in advance. A fixed quantity for a fixed price usually means automation or a network of sites that exist to sell links.
  • Do they guarantee rankings? Nobody outside Google can. Treat the promise as a warning.
  • Will they show you where the links will be before you pay? If the answer is no, assume you would not like it.
  • Do they talk about metrics or about readers? Scores such as “DA” and “DR” are estimates produced by private software companies. Google does not use them, and they can be inflated.
  • Is the price too low to pay a person? Writing to an editor and producing something worth publishing takes hours. A link that costs less than a cup of tea was not earned that way.

A note for Indian small businesses

For a firm with a small marketing budget, the cheap package is tempting precisely because it is cheap. The more useful comparison is with what the same money would do elsewhere: a professional photograph set for your listing, a well-written page for each service you offer, or a month of properly labelled advertising whose results you can measure.

Founders raising money for a young business should also know that investors and acquirers check where a company’s web traffic comes from. Traffic built on purchased links is a liability in that conversation.

It also helps to recognise how much low-quality web content is produced only to catch search traffic, as our report on a piece of software that does not appear to exist shows.
