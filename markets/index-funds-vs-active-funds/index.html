Index Fund vs Mutual Fund: What's the Difference, and Which Suits You?

Most index funds are mutual funds. The real choice is passive vs active. Compare costs, returns, risk and tax, with SPIVA India data and SEBI's new expense caps.

Editorial Desk Published 6 Nov 2026 Updated 18 Nov 2026, 12:35 am IST 9 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

“Should I buy an index fund or a mutual fund?” is one of the most common questions new investors ask. The question itself contains a small mix-up. In India, almost every index fund is a mutual fund. The real choice is between two styles of fund: one that simply copies a market index (passive), and one where a manager picks stocks to try to beat it (active).

That choice matters more than it looks. It decides how much you pay in fees every year, how much your returns depend on one person’s judgement, and how you should judge a fund’s performance.

Key points

  • An index fund is a type of mutual fund that tracks an index such as the Nifty 50 or Sensex. It does not try to beat the market.
  • Most other equity mutual funds are actively managed and charge higher fees for stock-picking.
  • Over 10 years to December 2025, roughly three in four active large-cap funds in India trailed their benchmark, according to S&P Dow Jones Indices’ SPIVA India scorecard.
  • From 1 April 2026, SEBI’s cap on the base expense ratio for index funds and ETFs is 0.90%. Many direct-plan index funds charge far less.
  • Tax treatment is the same for both: equity index funds and active equity funds are taxed as equity funds.

What is a mutual fund?

A mutual fund pools money from many investors and invests it according to a stated objective, in shares, bonds, gold or a mix. Each investor owns units, and the value of one unit is the net asset value (NAV), calculated every business day. In India, mutual funds are run by asset management companies (AMCs) and regulated by the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI).

“Mutual fund” describes the structure. It says nothing about how the money is invested. That is where active and passive come in.

What is an index fund?

An index fund is a mutual fund that holds the same stocks as a market index, in the same proportions. A Nifty 50 index fund owns the 50 companies in the Nifty 50, weighted as the index weights them. When the index changes its members, the fund changes too.

There is no stock-picking and no attempt to beat the index. The goal is to match its return, minus a small cost. The gap between the fund’s return and the index’s return is called tracking error (strictly, the variation in that gap is tracking error and the average shortfall is tracking difference). Lower is better.

Exchange-traded funds (ETFs) follow the same idea but trade on the stock exchange like a share during market hours, so you need a demat account to buy them. Index funds are bought and sold directly with the AMC at that day’s NAV, and they allow SIPs (systematic investment plans) easily.

What is an actively managed fund?

In an active fund, a fund manager and research team decide which stocks to buy, how much to hold and when to sell. The aim is to earn more than a benchmark index. Large-cap, flexi-cap, mid-cap, small-cap and ELSS (tax-saving) funds are mostly active.

You pay for that research through a higher expense ratio, the annual fee deducted from the fund’s assets. You never see it as a bill. It simply reduces the NAV every day.

Index fund vs active mutual fund at a glance

Index fund (passive) Active mutual fund
Aim Match an index Beat a benchmark index
Who picks stocks The index rules A fund manager
Typical cost Low Higher
Depends on manager’s skill No Yes
Main risk beyond the market Small tracking error Under-performing the benchmark
Portfolio Fully predictable Can change with the manager’s views
Tax on equity funds Same Same

Both carry full market risk. When the Nifty falls 20%, a Nifty index fund falls about 20% too. An index fund removes the risk of choosing a poor manager. It does not remove the risk of the market itself.

Do active funds beat the index?

Some do, in some years. The difficulty is that it is hard to know in advance which ones, and few keep doing it.

S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes a regular SPIVA India scorecard comparing active funds with their benchmarks. In its year-end 2025 report, about 76% of active large-cap funds had underperformed the S&P India LargeMidCap index over 10 years, as reported by several Indian outlets. The pattern was similar for mid- and small-cap funds and ELSS funds over 10 years. The mid-year 2026 scorecard found most categories behind their benchmarks again in the first half of 2026.

Two cautions are fair to add. Fund houses have criticised SPIVA’s choice of benchmark, since SEBI defines large-cap funds by the top 100 stocks while SPIVA compares them against a broader large- and mid-cap index. And results vary by period: in some years, especially in mid- and small-caps, a majority of active funds have done better. Still, across long periods, the evidence points the same way. The market is hard to beat consistently once fees are deducted.

Why costs matter so much

Fees compound just like returns. Take ₹10 lakh invested for 20 years at a gross return of 12% a year:

Annual cost Value after 20 years (approx.)
0.2% ₹93 lakh
1.0% ₹81 lakh
1.5% ₹74 lakh

Illustration only. Returns are not guaranteed and will vary year to year.

The gap between a 0.2% fund and a 1.5% fund is about ₹19 lakh, money that went to costs rather than to the investor. An active fund has to beat the market by more than its extra fee every year just to match a cheap index fund.

What SEBI allows. Under SEBI’s revised expense framework, in force from 1 April 2026, the cap on the base expense ratio for index funds and ETFs fell to 0.90% from 1.00%. Active equity funds have higher caps that fall as a scheme grows larger. Statutory levies such as GST and stamp duty are now charged on top of the base ratio, so the total you pay can be slightly higher than the base figure. These are ceilings. Many direct-plan index funds tracking the Nifty 50 charge a small fraction of the cap.

Direct vs regular plans. Every fund, active or passive, comes in two versions. A regular plan includes commission for a distributor. A direct plan, bought from the AMC or a direct platform, does not, and costs less. Over decades, choosing the direct plan of the same fund can make a large difference.

Passive investing is growing fast in India

Indian investors have been moving towards passive funds. Industry data based on AMFI figures shows passive assets, index funds and ETFs together, rising from about ₹9 lakh crore in March 2024 to roughly ₹13.7 lakh crore by March 2026, with index funds the fastest-growing part. Much of the ETF money comes from institutions such as the Employees’ Provident Fund Organisation. Active funds still hold the large majority of mutual fund assets.

You can check any fund’s assets, returns and expense ratio for free on the Association of Mutual Funds in India (AMFI) website and in each fund’s monthly factsheet.

How tax works

Tax depends on what a fund holds, not on whether it is active or passive. A Nifty 50 index fund and an active large-cap fund are both equity funds, taxed the same way under the rules in force since 23 July 2024:

  • Units held up to 12 months: short-term capital gains taxed at 20%.
  • Units held over 12 months: long-term gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year taxed at 12.5%.

Debt index funds and gold funds are taxed differently. One practical advantage of index funds is lower portfolio turnover, which keeps trading costs inside the fund low. Check current rules with a tax adviser before acting, as budgets can change them.

Which suits you?

An index fund may suit you if:

  • you are starting out and want one simple, low-cost core holding;
  • you do not want to research and monitor fund managers;
  • you are investing for the long term through a SIP and are happy with market returns.

An active fund may suit you if:

  • you are willing to study a manager’s record over five years or more and check it regularly;
  • you accept that the fund may lag the index for long stretches;
  • you are investing in segments such as small-caps, where active managers are often argued to have more room to add value, though the SPIVA data shows many still fall short.

Many investors use both: an index fund as the core of the portfolio and one or two carefully chosen active funds around it. What matters most is keeping costs low, staying invested and not chasing last year’s best performer.

How to compare funds before you invest

  1. Pick the direct plan and compare expense ratios within the same category.
  2. For index funds, compare tracking difference and tracking error across funds on the same index. Also check fund size, since very small funds can track less closely.
  3. For active funds, compare returns with the benchmark over five and ten years, not one year. Check whether the manager who produced the record is still running the fund.
  4. Read the scheme information document for the objective, risk level (shown on the riskometer) and exit load.

Frequently asked questions

Is an index fund a mutual fund? In India, yes. An index fund is a mutual fund scheme that follows an index passively. ETFs are also passive but trade on the exchange.

Are index funds safer than active funds? They are not safer from market falls. They remove the risk of a poor fund manager and usually cost less, but their value moves with the index.

Can I start a SIP in an index fund? Yes. Index funds accept SIPs like any other mutual fund, often from ₹100 to ₹500 a month depending on the AMC.

Which index should a beginner choose? Many beginners start with a broad large-cap index such as the Nifty 50 or Sensex. Broader indices such as the Nifty 500 add mid- and small-caps and more risk.

Mutual fund investments are subject to market risks. Read all scheme-related documents carefully. Past performance does not guarantee future returns.
