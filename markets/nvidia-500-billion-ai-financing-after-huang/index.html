Nvidia's $500 Billion AI Financing Plan After Huang: Who Still Gets Funded, and Who May Not

Nvidia's partners say they will keep funding AI infrastructure after Jensen Huang's exit, but lenders want safer terms. Here is how the financing works and who is exposed.

Editorial Desk Published 16 Nov 2026, 8:09 pm IST 4 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

Jensen Huang did more than run Nvidia. He was also the person who persuaded banks, investment firms and customers to finance the enormous build-out of AI data centres that keeps demand for Nvidia’s chips growing.

His resignation on 12 November has left investors asking whether that money keeps flowing. The early answer, according to an analysis by Reuters, is yes, but on more cautious terms. The riskiest borrowers are the ones most likely to feel the difference.

Key points

  • Nvidia announced a $500 billion financing platform in August with six large investment firms. It has not yet announced a deal.
  • Separately, Bank of America estimates Nvidia holds about $70 billion of stakes in AI companies and has given about $230 billion of financial guarantees.
  • Partners say they intend to continue. Analysts expect money to shift towards larger, safer customers.
  • Lenders now want loans backed by customer contracts, not by the chips themselves.

How Nvidia’s financing works

There are three layers, and it helps to keep them apart.

The $500 billion platform. Huang unveiled this in August, partly to answer criticism that Nvidia was financing purchases of its own products. The idea was to bring in outside institutional money, with Nvidia limiting any guarantee to a maximum of 25%. The partners are Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield Asset Management, Goldman Sachs and KKR. Huang had proposed that Nvidia’s chips could serve as collateral for the loans.

Equity stakes. Outside the platform, Nvidia owns shares in AI companies, including the cloud provider CoreWeave and a number of startups. Bank of America put these at about $70 billion as of August.

Guarantees. Nvidia has promised to stand behind certain obligations of its customers. Bank of America estimated these at about $230 billion. The largest reported example is a guarantee of up to $105 billion to help OpenAI lease a data centre in Ohio.

A guarantee is not a loan. It is a promise to pay if someone else cannot. It costs nothing while things go well and can become very expensive if they do not.

What partners and analysts are saying

The firms involved have been careful to signal continuity.

“We greatly value the relationship between our teams and look forward to continuing our work together,” said Sikander Rashid of Brookfield.

Analysts are more guarded.

  • Brian Colello of Morningstar said the change of chief executive “adds additional uncertainty”.
  • Erica Spear of JPMorgan said “new deals could take longer or require more compensation”.
  • Joseph Moore of Morgan Stanley said the platform’s money “is more likely to go to more established players”, meaning the largest cloud companies.

Two people familiar with the discussions told Reuters that the partners are still talking about deals. There could be a pause while Nvidia and the financiers consider the platform’s direction, though one source said Nvidia had not signalled one.

Who is most exposed

The companies with most to lose are the so-called neoclouds: newer firms that buy Nvidia chips, install them in data centres and rent out the computing power. They have little history, heavy debts and a business that depends on continued demand from AI developers.

Under Huang, Nvidia was willing to back them, by guaranteeing part of their early investment in return for a share of revenue. Morgan Stanley still expects neoclouds to account for about a quarter of Nvidia’s revenue. If financing for them tightens, that is the part of Nvidia’s sales most at risk.

AI startups that rely on Nvidia’s investment are in a similar position.

The largest technology companies are not. They fund data centres from their own profits and can borrow cheaply.

What lenders want now

The most telling change is in the terms. Lenders and credit investors now want future financing secured against the borrower’s customer contracts or backed by financial guarantees, Reuters reported, and are less willing to accept the chips as collateral.

The reason is practical. A chip loses value quickly as newer models arrive. A signed contract from a creditworthy customer is worth more to a lender than a warehouse of processors that may be outdated in three years.

One credit investor put the logic simply. “As long as they keep producing compute, we get repaid by the parties leasing that capacity,” said Edwin Wilches of the asset manager PGIM.

Three pressures arriving together

  • Leadership. The dealmaker has gone, and the interim chief executive comes from the finance side.
  • Interest rates. Borrowing costs are rising, which reduces appetite for new deals.
  • Politics. Democrats, who take control of Congress in January, have been critical of the pace of data-centre construction.

The head of the International Monetary Fund added a fourth this week, warning that circular financing in AI has passed $1 trillion.

What this means for investors

The lesson is about where risk sits. Much of the recent AI build-out has been paid for with borrowed money and guarantees that do not appear in a company’s headline profit. They are found in the notes to the accounts, under headings such as commitments and contingencies. Our guide to reading quarterly results explains where to look.

For Indian investors holding US technology funds, the practical questions are how much of the fund is in companies tied to this financing, and whether the fund’s recent returns depended on it continuing.

Nvidia reports its quarterly results on 17 November. What it says about guarantees, and about its willingness to keep funding customers, will matter as much as the profit figure.
