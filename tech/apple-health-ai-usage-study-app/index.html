Apple Launches a Study App to Test How People Use AI for Health Questions: What It Does and Who Can Join

Apple wants up to 200,000 US iPhone users to ask an AI health questions and rate the answers. Here is how the study works, what data it collects and why it matters.

Editorial Desk Published 16 Nov 2026, 9:39 am IST 4 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

Apple has released an app whose purpose is research: to find out what happens when ordinary people ask an artificial intelligence system about their health.

The Apple Health AI Usage Study, announced on 15 November, lets participants ask medical questions and receive answers drawn from clinical journals. In return, they rate the answers and fill in surveys. Apple hopes to enrol up to 200,000 people over more than a year, TechCrunch reported.

It is a cautious move from a company that has so far kept generative AI out of its Health app, and it comes as rivals move faster.

Key points

  • The study is open to people in the United States aged 21 and over.
  • Answers are provided through OpenEvidence, an AI company that builds tools for doctors, and are based on sources such as The New England Journal of Medicine and JAMA.
  • Apple says the data will not be used to train AI models.
  • Apple has not said whether AI answers will later come to the Health app or Siri.

How the study works

A participant downloads the app and asks a health question in plain language. The app returns a simplified answer grounded in published clinical sources.

The participant then does the work the study is designed around:

  • rates each answer and gives feedback
  • answers a survey each month
  • can choose to share data from Apple’s HealthKit, such as resting heart rate, activity, sleep or laboratory results, to receive more personal suggestions

The app can also help someone prepare questions for a doctor’s appointment, and points users towards a healthcare provider when an issue looks concerning.

According to Apple, the aim is to understand how people engage with health AI, whether they understand what it tells them, and how they judge it. “Health is deeply personal,” said Sumbul Desai, Apple’s vice president of health and fitness, who called a dedicated research study “the most thoughtful way” to learn this.

Who can take part

Requirement Detail
Location United States
Age 21 or older
Device iPhone 15 or later, according to TechCrunch
Software iOS 26 or later, with Apple Intelligence switched on
Places Up to 200,000
Length More than 12 months

Reports differ slightly on which iPhone models qualify, so the app’s own listing is the place to check.

What happens to the data

Apple’s description of the privacy terms, as reported, has three parts:

  • data is anonymised before it is sent to a secure cloud provider
  • it will not be used to train AI models
  • OpenEvidence deletes each question and answer after 24 hours

Those are Apple’s statements, and they have not been independently audited. Anyone joining should read the consent form, as with any health study. Sharing HealthKit data is optional.

Why Apple is doing it this way

People are already asking chatbots about their health in very large numbers. OpenAI has said its users send more than 300 million health-related queries a week, TechCrunch noted, and both OpenAI and Anthropic have launched health-focused features.

Apple has taken the slower route before. Its Apple Heart Study enrolled more than 400,000 people before the company expanded the heart features on its watch. A study gives it evidence, and regulatory cover, before it puts medical answers in front of more than a billion device users.

The design also reveals what Apple thinks the hard problem is. Getting an AI to produce a plausible answer is easy. Knowing whether a person understood it, trusted it too much or too little, and did something sensible afterwards is much harder. That is what the ratings and surveys measure.

The risks the study is meant to explore

  • Wrong or outdated answers. Grounding answers in journals reduces invented claims. It does not remove errors of interpretation.
  • False reassurance. A calm, fluent answer can discourage someone from seeing a doctor when they should.
  • Unequal results. Advice drawn mainly from studies of some populations may fit others less well.
  • Privacy. Health questions are among the most sensitive data a person generates.

What it means for users in India

The study is limited to the US, so Indian iPhone users cannot join. The questions it raises apply everywhere.

Many people in India already turn to chatbots for health information, often because a doctor is far away or expensive. A few habits make that safer:

  • use AI answers to prepare for a consultation, not to replace one
  • never start, stop or change a medicine on the strength of a chatbot’s reply
  • be careful about typing in names, report numbers or identity details
  • treat sudden or severe symptoms as a reason to seek care at once

For Indian digital health companies, Apple’s approach offers a model worth noting: test with a defined group, measure understanding and outcomes, and publish what is learned before scaling up.

What to watch

  • whether Apple publishes results, and when
  • whether the answers move into the Health app or Siri
  • how regulators treat AI tools that stop short of diagnosis but still influence health decisions
