Nvidia After Jensen Huang: How the CEO's Exit Is Reshaping the US-China AI Chip Fight

Jensen Huang has resigned as Nvidia CEO days before Trump meets Xi in Shenzhen. Here is what is confirmed, what is only alleged, and what it means for AI chip exports.

Editorial Desk Published 15 Nov 2026, 2:14 pm IST 6 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

Nvidia, the company whose chips power most of the world’s artificial intelligence, is without its founder-CEO for the first time in its history. Jensen Huang resigned on Thursday, 12 November, and the timing could hardly be more awkward: US President Donald Trump is due to meet Chinese President Xi Jinping in Shenzhen this week, with AI chips already one of the hardest issues between the two countries.

The resignation has turned a corporate crisis into a foreign policy one. Here is what is confirmed, what remains an allegation, and what could change for the chip trade.

Key points

  • Nvidia said on 12 November that Huang had stepped down as president, chief executive and director with immediate effect. Chief financial officer Colette Kress is interim CEO.
  • US media reports say American officials suspect a woman Huang was involved with of working for Chinese intelligence. This has not been proven, and Beijing rejects it.
  • US lawmakers from both parties are using the episode to push for tighter limits on AI chip sales to China.
  • US officials say Trump does not plan to raise the matter with Xi at the APEC summit on 18 and 19 November.

What is confirmed

Nvidia announced Huang’s departure on Thursday. Alongside Kress’s interim appointment, the board named Harvey Jones, a long-serving director and former Synopsys chief executive, as its first chair, CNBC reported. A search for a permanent CEO is under way.

The shares fell as much as 14% during Thursday’s session before recovering to close 4.34% lower at $166.19. Bernstein halved its price target on the stock from $400 to $200, and HSBC cut its rating to hold on Friday. The stock had already lost more than a fifth of its value over the previous month.

Nvidia’s board opened an independent investigation on 8 November. It said at the time that it had no reason to believe national security or company secrets had been compromised. The company has not publicly linked Huang’s resignation to any specific allegation.

What is alleged, and what Beijing says

The resignation followed a week of reports in the Financial Times, The New York Times and Reuters about Huang’s personal relationships, including one with an executive who, according to the Times, reported to him.

The report with the widest consequences concerns a former executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based cloud computing company that buys Nvidia hardware. According to those reports, US officials suspect she was working for Chinese intelligence and may have been in a position to influence Nvidia’s stance on selling chips to China.

That is a suspicion attributed to unnamed officials. No charges have been announced and no evidence has been made public. China has called the claim unsubstantiated. “The US should stop fabricating the ‘Chinese spy’ narratives as a pretext for imposing technology restrictions,” foreign ministry spokesperson Mao Ning said on Thursday, according to the Associated Press.

The chairs of three US House committees have asked for a closed-door briefing, and Huang has reportedly agreed to testify before Congress.

Why one company matters so much

Training the most capable AI models needs enormous numbers of specialised processors, and Nvidia makes the best of them. That has made its chips a strategic asset as much as a commercial product.

The US began restricting sales of advanced chips to China in October 2022, arguing that they could strengthen the Chinese military. Beijing says Washington is trying to keep a monopoly on AI. Nvidia was caught in the middle. It once held about 95% of China’s AI chip market, the AP noted, and its sales there have collapsed.

Huang spent much of the past two years arguing in Washington that the limits would only push China to build its own chip industry faster. He had some success. In December 2025 Trump said Nvidia could sell its H200 chip, which is advanced but not the company’s most powerful, to approved Chinese customers. The US Commerce Department moved those applications to case-by-case review in January.

Very little has actually shipped. Nvidia’s filing for the quarter that ended on 26 July said licensed sales to China amounted to less than 1% of its data centre revenue, with Chinese government restrictions also holding back orders.

What changes now

The most immediate effect is political. Huang was the most persuasive voice in Washington for keeping some chip trade with China open, and he spoke to Trump regularly. That voice is gone, and the people who want tighter controls have new momentum.

“China hawks now have a story with a name and a face,” Shaoyu Yuan of New York University told the AP.

Several proposals are already moving:

  • A group of congressional Democrats has written to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick asking what officials knew and calling for an immediate halt to advanced AI chip exports to China.
  • The Senate’s version of the annual defence policy bill would let Congress review licences for advanced chip sales to China and require the Commerce Department to track chips so they are not diverted.
  • The Remote Access Security Act, which the House passed 369 to 22 in January, would allow curbs on foreign access to restricted chips through cloud services. It is still pending in the Senate.

None of these is law yet. But the direction is clear, and Nvidia no longer has its chief lobbyist.

The Trump-Xi meeting

Trump and Xi are expected to meet on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation leaders’ summit in Shenzhen on 18 and 19 November. Trade, rare earths and farm purchases lead the agenda.

Senior US officials, speaking anonymously, told the AP that Trump does not intend to confront Xi over the Nvidia matter. They also said they do not expect China to retaliate if Washington closes loopholes that allow advanced chips to be smuggled or used remotely.

The risk lies in the quieter work. The two governments agreed in September to hold a regular dialogue on AI and have backed the idea of a hotline for AI-related security incidents. Kyle Chan of the Brookings Institution told the AP that the scandal and any new export controls could disrupt those talks, depending on whether Beijing chooses to link the issues.

What it means for China’s chip industry

Analysts say Nvidia’s top processors still beat Chinese alternatives chip for chip, particularly for training large models. Chinese companies have narrowed the gap by linking large numbers of less advanced domestic chips together and by focusing on cheaper, open-source models.

Tighter US rules would speed that up. Rui Ma of Tech Buzz China told the AP the episode could push Beijing to accelerate its domestic semiconductor supply chain, which is the outcome Huang warned about.

Why Indian readers should watch this

India is a buyer in this market, not a bystander. Reliance and the Tata Group both announced AI infrastructure partnerships with Nvidia in 2023, and Indian data centre operators and the government’s IndiaAI Mission depend on a steady supply of its processors.

Two things matter from here. First, any new US rule that tracks where chips end up will add compliance work for buyers everywhere, including India. Second, Nvidia is one of the most widely held US stocks among Indian investors who buy overseas shares or international funds. A leadership change at a company this dependent on one person is a reminder of why spreading money across a whole index lowers the damage any single stock can do.

Nvidia’s next quarterly results will be the first test of how the company communicates without Huang, and a good moment to apply the basics of reading a results announcement: look at the guidance and the commentary on China, and not only the headline profit.

What to watch next

  • 18 and 19 November: the APEC summit and the Trump-Xi meeting in Shenzhen.
  • Congress: Huang’s testimony and the fate of the chip provisions in the defence bill.
  • Nvidia’s board: the outcome of its investigation and the choice of a permanent CEO.
  • Commerce Department: any change to H200 licences for Chinese buyers.

It draws on reporting by the Associated Press, CNBC, the Financial Times, The New York Times, Reuters and Outlook India, and on Nvidia’s public statements. The intelligence allegations described here are unproven and are denied by the Chinese government.
