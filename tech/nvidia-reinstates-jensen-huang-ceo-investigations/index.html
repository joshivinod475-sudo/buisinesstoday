Nvidia Reinstates Jensen Huang as CEO 11 Days After He Resigned, but Investigations Continue

Nvidia's board reversed course and brought Jensen Huang back as chief executive after pressure from President Trump, investors and staff. Here is what changed, and the probes that remain.

Editorial Desk Published 24 Nov 2026, 8:15 pm IST 3 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

Nvidia has reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive, 11 days after he resigned. He had stepped down following reports of extramarital relationships, including one with a woman whom US officials reportedly believe to be a Chinese spy.

The board announced on Monday, 23 November, that it had voted unanimously to bring him back after “a series of discussions and meetings” with him. The decision reversed the board’s position of 17 November, when new chair Harvey Jones rejected calls for Huang’s return.

President Donald Trump said he had personally intervened. “I made the deal,” he told CNBC.

Key points

  • Huang returns as CEO of the world’s most valuable chipmaker 11 days after resigning.
  • Nvidia’s shares rose 31% on Monday, adding nearly $1 trillion in market value, before falling about 6% on Tuesday.
  • Trump said the government gave the board assurances that Huang is not a national security risk.
  • Nvidia’s own investigation into whether Huang was targeted by foreign intelligence is continuing.
  • California’s Department of Justice and, according to Reuters, the SEC are also looking at the company.

How the reversal happened

Date Event
12 November Huang resigns as CEO after reports about his relationships
Days later Huang offers to return; the board initially refuses
17 November Board chair Harvey Jones rejects calls to reinstate him
Following week Campaign by investors and employees; Trump speaks to the board
23 November Board unanimously reinstates Huang

Trump said the board had asked for guarantees that Huang was not a threat to national security. “We gave them assurances, at least with everything we know,” he said. On Truth Social, he wrote that the government had “no reason to believe” Huang’s return posed a security risk and did “not possess evidence that he has engaged in any unlawful conduct”.

Investors and staff had campaigned for his return, including through a petition calling for Huang to come back.

The investigations that remain

  • Nvidia’s internal review into whether Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort. The company said this would continue.
  • California’s Department of Justice is examining whether Nvidia’s advanced chips or confidential information reached China and whether investors were misled.
  • The SEC is looking into the company’s accounting practices, Reuters reported. The SEC declined to comment.
  • Congress: Huang is due to sit for a closed-door interview with three House committees on 1 December and to testify publicly before the Senate Banking Committee on 3 December. We covered those plans in our report on Huang’s Senate testimony.

Separately, US authorities are investigating Alice Huang and Megaspeed, the company where she worked, over whether Nvidia chips reached China despite export controls. China has rejected the espionage allegations as baseless.

What analysts say

Analysts welcomed the end of the leadership uncertainty but warned that the governance questions remain.

  • William Blair’s Sebastien Naji said the return “clears some of the uncertainty” but that recent weeks “exposed deeper questions around corporate governance, leadership succession, and regulatory oversight”.
  • StoneX’s Cody Acree called the board’s investigation the “key risk”. Any finding that “confidential information was shared” would “reopen the leadership question”, he wrote.
  • Bank of America said: “Reinstatement should not be confused with the end of scrutiny.”

Why it matters

Nvidia supplies most of the chips used to train and run AI models and finances many companies across the AI industry. Its leadership crisis contributed to the Nasdaq’s worst week since 2020. Huang’s return calmed markets, but it also raised questions about board independence and the role of political influence in corporate decisions. We look at the political fallout in our report on Democrats’ response to Trump’s intervention.
