Nvidia's Board After Jensen Huang: A First-Ever Chair, a CEO Search and Hard Questions on Oversight

Nvidia's directors accepted Jensen Huang's resignation, named their first chair and began a CEO search. Here is what the filings show and what the board has not said.

Editorial Desk Published 15 Nov 2026, 6:46 pm IST 7 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

For 33 years Nvidia’s board had one job that it never had to do: replace Jensen Huang. This week it had to do it in a few days, under pressure from investors, lawmakers and the press.

The directors accepted Huang’s resignation on 11 November, put the finance chief in charge on an interim basis and created a post the company had never had, a chair of the board. The spotlight has now moved from the former chief executive to the ten people who oversee the world’s most valuable chipmaker, and to whether they were ready for this.

Here is what the board has done, what the official filings show, and what remains unanswered.

Key points

  • Nvidia’s board accepted Huang’s resignation as president, chief executive and director on 11 November and announced it the next day.
  • Harvey Jones, a director since 1993, is Nvidia’s first board chair. Chief financial officer Colette Kress is interim chief executive and keeps her finance role.
  • The company’s regulatory filing gives no reason for the resignation and no details of any exit terms.
  • A board investigation, run with an outside law firm, is still under way.
  • Nvidia reports quarterly results on 17 November, the first under the new leadership.

What the board has done so far

The sequence, drawn from company statements and reporting by CNBC, looks like this:

Date What happened
5 November Three US House committee chairs ask Huang for a closed-door briefing
8 November Huang acknowledges an extramarital relationship in an email to staff. The board opens an independent investigation
10 November The board says it has hired the law firm Wachtell Lipton to run the investigation
11 November The board meets and accepts Huang’s resignation
12 November Nvidia announces the change. Shares fall as much as 14% and close 4.3% lower
17 November Third-quarter results due
18 November Company-wide staff meeting

The investigation concerns allegations, reported by The New York Times and the Financial Times, that Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort. Those allegations are unproven and China denies them. When it opened the inquiry, the board said it had “no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised”. We covered that side of the story, and its effect on chip exports, in our report on how Huang’s exit is reshaping the US-China AI chip fight.

What the filing says, and what it leaves out

Listed US companies must report the departure of a chief executive to the Securities and Exchange Commission. Nvidia’s filing is brief.

It confirms that Huang resigned from all three of his roles and that the board accepted the resignation “effective immediately” on 11 November. It names Kress, 59, as interim chief executive while the board searches for a successor, and Jones, 73, as chair.

It does not say why Huang resigned. It does not say whether he will receive any severance payment, what happens to share awards that have not yet vested, or whether Kress will be paid more for the added role. Companies often disclose such terms in a later filing, so more may follow.

The accompanying press release also gives no reason. It says the search will consider candidates from inside and outside the company and sets no deadline.

Who is in charge now

Colette Kress has been Nvidia’s chief financial officer since 2013, after finance roles at Cisco and Microsoft. Investors know her well because she presents the numbers on every results call. In an email to staff seen by CNBC, she acknowledged an “extraordinarily difficult period” and said she believed deeply in the company’s future.

Harvey Jones joined the board in 1993, the year Nvidia was founded, and once ran the chip-design software company Synopsys. He told employees the board had “full confidence” in Kress and expected operations and the product roadmap to continue without interruption.

Until this week Nvidia had no chair at all. Board meetings were coordinated by a lead independent director, a role held by lawyer Stephen Neal according to the company’s latest proxy statement, while Huang set the direction as chief executive.

The criticism: a long-serving board and no visible successor

The board is now being asked why a company worth trillions of dollars had no public succession plan.

Jeffrey Sonnenfeld, a Yale management professor who studies chief executives, told CNBC that Nvidia had a “monarch” problem and that “no one was equipped to step in”. He pointed to the make-up of the board: the directors other than Huang have served for about 15 years on average, roughly double the 7.8-year average for independent directors at large US companies, according to Conference Board data he cited. None is a current or former chief executive of an S&P 500 company.

Long tenure cuts both ways. Directors who have been there for decades know the business deeply. They may also find it harder to challenge the founder who brought them on.

There is a counter-argument. Some shareholders think the board moved too fast. Investor Gavin Baker argued publicly that directors should have waited for the investigation’s findings before letting Huang go. A person familiar with the discussions told CNBC that sentiment on the board shifted after further reports about Huang’s conduct, including a relationship with an executive who reported to him. Cantor Fitzgerald analyst CJ Muse said the uncertainty around the inquiry and possible congressional investigations made it “incredibly difficult” for the board to keep him.

The search for a permanent CEO

The board has named no shortlist. The names in circulation come from analysts and press reports, and should be read as speculation:

  • Inside the company: Kress herself, and senior technologists including Ian Buck, who built Nvidia’s data centre software business, and chief scientist Bill Dally. Ben Bajarin of Creative Strategies told CNBC that Buck was the most logical permanent choice.
  • Outside: analyst Dan Ives said Kress could steady the company but that the long-term chief executive would probably come from elsewhere. The Financial Times has reported interest in Arm chief executive Rene Haas, and other reports have mentioned AMD’s Lisa Su.

Whoever is chosen inherits a company whose customers, partners and engineers were used to dealing with one person.

Huang is gone from the board but still a major shareholder

Huang’s stake in Nvidia was about 3.6% at the time of the company’s 2026 proxy statement, according to an analysis of that filing, and is worth roughly $140 billion. CNBC reported that much of it sits in trusts held jointly with his wife, which lawyers say makes a quick sale less likely.

Nvidia has a single class of shares, so Huang has no special voting rights. He is a large shareholder but cannot overrule the board. That is a real difference from founder-led companies such as Meta or Alphabet, where special share classes give founders control regardless of what directors think.

What else the board is facing

  • Congress. House Foreign Affairs chair Brian Mast said Huang has agreed to testify, with a date still being arranged. Senate Banking chair Tim Scott has asked him to appear on 3 December.
  • Lawsuits. A US law firm has filed a proposed class action on behalf of investors who lost money. Ann Lipton, a law professor at the University of Colorado, told CNBC the resignation could strengthen shareholders’ claims.
  • The investigation. Its findings will show whether the board’s early statement that no secrets were compromised holds up.

Why this matters for Indian investors

The Nvidia episode is a textbook case of what analysts call key-person risk: a company so closely tied to one individual that their departure changes how the market values it.

India has many companies like that, led by founders or promoter families. Indian rules do ask for some protection. SEBI’s listing regulations require boards to satisfy themselves that succession plans exist for directors and senior management. A proposal to force large companies to separate the roles of chairperson and managing director was made voluntary in 2022.

Rules only go so far. For anyone who owns shares, here or abroad, three questions are worth asking about a company:

  1. Is the chair independent of the chief executive?
  2. How long have the independent directors served?
  3. Has the company named, or visibly groomed, a successor?

The answers are in the annual report. Our guide to reading a company’s quarterly results covers where to look, and the case for spreading money across an index is strongest when a single stock depends on a single person.

What to watch next

  • 17 November: third-quarter results and the first earnings call led by Kress.
  • Further filings: any disclosure of Huang’s exit terms or Kress’s pay.
  • The Wachtell Lipton investigation: its findings and whether they are made public.
  • The CEO search: whether the board sets a timeline, and whether it looks inside or outside.
