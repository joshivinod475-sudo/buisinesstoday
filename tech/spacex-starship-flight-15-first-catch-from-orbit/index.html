SpaceX Catches Starship From Orbit for the First Time: What Flight 15 Means for the Space Business

SpaceX caught its Starship upper stage at the launch tower after 14 orbits, a first for any rocket, but lost the booster. Here is what happened and why it matters.

Editorial Desk Published 15 Nov 2026, 4:24 pm IST Updated 15 Nov 2026, 4:31 pm IST 6 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

SpaceX has brought a spacecraft back from orbit and caught it in mid-air at its own launch pad, something no company or space agency had done before.

The upper stage of its giant Starship rocket returned to the Starbase site in Texas on Saturday, 14 November, after about 14 trips around the Earth, and was grabbed by the mechanical arms of the launch tower. The flight was not a complete success. The booster that lifted it off the ground was lost over the Gulf of Mexico minutes after launch.

Here is what happened on Starship’s 15th test flight and why it matters for the business of space.

Key points

  • Starship lifted off from Starbase on Friday evening, 13 November, US time.
  • The upper stage, known as the ship, reached orbit, released 26 Starlink satellites and was caught by the tower at 4:21 pm US Eastern time on Saturday, about 22 and a half hours after launch.
  • The Super Heavy booster missed its own planned catch. It came down over the Gulf of Mexico and was destroyed.
  • SpaceX says it is still investigating what went wrong with the booster.
  • NASA administrator Jared Isaacman praised the catch. NASA is paying SpaceX to build a Starship that will land astronauts on the Moon.

What happened on the flight

Starship has two parts. The Super Heavy booster provides the push off the pad and separates after a few minutes. The ship on top carries the cargo into orbit.

SpaceX planned to catch both parts with the tower’s arms, which the company calls “chopsticks”. It would have been the first double catch.

The ship worked. It reached an orbit about 275 km up and released 26 of the company’s new, larger Starlink V3 internet satellites. Three of them carried cameras that photographed the ship’s heat shield, the layer of tiles that protects it from the heat of re-entry. SpaceX said the pictures showed the shield was in good condition, so it went ahead with the return to Texas and did not use the backup option of landing in the sea.

After roughly 14 orbits the ship fired its engines, re-entered the atmosphere and descended to the tower, where the arms closed around it. NBC News described it as a dramatic first.

The booster did not. About six and a half minutes after lift-off, the booster abandoned its return to the tower and diverted over the Gulf of Mexico, where its automatic self-destruct system was triggered.

Why the booster was lost

SpaceX has not confirmed a root cause, and its account has shifted since the launch.

  • During the launch broadcast, SpaceX commentators said the booster had judged itself out of position and aborted automatically, and that engines had cut out early on the way down.
  • Chief executive Elon Musk said on X that the booster ran short of propellant because its engines slightly underperformed. He said the fixes would include more consistent engines and a software change to shut them down about a second earlier.
  • SpaceX later said the booster’s guidance and navigation system had in fact worked properly and was not the cause, Space.com reported. Engineers are still studying the data.

It was also unclear at the time of writing whether the US Federal Aviation Administration would require a formal mishap investigation. After some earlier Starship failures, the regulator grounded the rocket until an inquiry was finished.

Musk said the loss “does not change our timeline to reach high volume, fully reusable Starship flights”.

It is worth remembering that the abort itself is a safety feature. The booster is programmed to stay away from the launch site unless everything checks out, because a failed catch could destroy the tower.

Why catching the ship matters

Rockets have traditionally been thrown away after one use. SpaceX changed that with its Falcon 9, whose booster lands and flies again, and that is the main reason it now launches more cargo than the rest of the world combined. But Falcon 9’s upper stage is still lost on every flight.

Starship is designed so that both parts come back and fly again quickly, like an aircraft. Super Heavy boosters have been caught three times before. Until Saturday, no ship had come back from orbit to the launch site.

If SpaceX can repeat it and reuse the ship, the cost of putting each kilogram into orbit should fall sharply. That matters for three lines of business:

  • Starlink. The 26 satellites on this flight carry about as much capacity as 10 Falcon 9 launches of the older model, according to SpaceX. Cheaper launches mean a faster, cheaper network.
  • NASA’s Moon programme. A version of Starship is the lander for the Artemis missions. That plan depends on refuelling Starship in orbit with several tanker flights, which is only practical if the ships are reusable. NASA administrator Jared Isaacman called the catch “almost magic”, according to US media reports.
  • Computing in orbit. SpaceX has talked about building data centres in space, which would need very large amounts of cargo launched cheaply.

One catch does not prove any of this. SpaceX still has to show that a caught ship can fly again without lengthy repairs, and that it can do so routinely. Musk has said he expects weekly Starship launches in the first half of 2027. His timelines have often slipped.

What it means for SpaceX investors

SpaceX has been a listed company since June, when it sold shares at $135 each. The stock closed at $139.11 on Friday, before the launch, which values the company at around $1.9 trillion.

The shares slipped about 2% in after-hours trading on Friday once it became clear the booster had been lost. The ship was caught on Saturday, when markets were closed, so Monday, 16 November, will be the first full session to reflect both outcomes.

Two cautions apply. Launch services are a small part of SpaceX’s income: space accounted for $1.8 billion of its $14.6 billion in third-quarter revenue, according to Motley Fool, with most of the rest from Starlink and artificial intelligence. And analysts disagree widely on whether the valuation is justified. A share price built on long-term promises can move sharply on a single test flight, in either direction. Indian investors who hold US shares through overseas accounts or international funds should treat it that way, and it is one more argument for spreading money across an index over betting on one company.

Why this matters in India

Satellite internet. Starlink holds a licence from the Department of Telecommunications and a clearance from the space regulator IN-SPACe, but it has not started selling service in India. Recent reports say it is still waiting for spectrum to be assigned and for final security clearance, and it has not announced Indian prices. Bigger satellites launched at lower cost would let it serve more customers per launch when it does arrive, in a market where Reliance Jio and Bharti Airtel have satellite broadband plans of their own.

Launch competition. India’s space agency ISRO has built its commercial reputation on low-cost launches. Fully reusable rockets move the goalposts. ISRO is developing its own Next Generation Launch Vehicle with a reusable first stage, approved by the government in 2024, and India’s private sector is now in the race too: Hyderabad-based Skyroot reached orbit with its Vikram-1 rocket in July this year, and Chennai’s Agnikul is working towards its first orbital launch. These are small rockets built for small satellites, a different market from Starship’s. But every launch provider will be measured against a much lower price per kilogram if Starship delivers.

Startups. Cheaper access to orbit helps companies that build satellites more than those that build rockets. For Indian space startups raising money, that shifts where the opportunity lies.

What to watch next

  • The booster investigation, including whether the FAA orders a formal inquiry before the next flight.
  • Reuse: whether the caught ship flies again, and how soon.
  • Monday’s trading in SpaceX shares.
  • Orbital refuelling tests, the next big step for NASA’s Moon lander.
