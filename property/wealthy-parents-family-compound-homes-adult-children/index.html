Bowling Alleys and Bunk Rooms: Why Wealthy Parents Are Building Homes to Bring Adult Children Back

Affluent American parents are adding bunk rooms, guest cottages and even bowling alleys so grown-up children keep visiting. Here is what is driving the trend.

Editorial Desk Published 15 Nov 2026, 7:03 pm IST 4 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

For most parents, the question is how to afford the children’s education. For a growing number of wealthy American parents, it is how to get those children, now adults with their own lives, to keep coming home.

Their answer is to build. The Wall Street Journal reported this week that well-off parents are spending heavily on houses designed to draw adult children and grandchildren back. A Fox Business segment on the same theme described home bowling alleys costing around $100,000 and basements turned into arcades.

Architects and designers say it is more than a fad. Here is what they are building, and why.

Key points

  • Designers report rising demand for family compounds: a main house with guest cottages and shared leisure spaces.
  • A record 17% of US homes bought last year were multigenerational, according to the National Association of Realtors.
  • About one in three Americans aged 18 to 34 lives with their parents, a study by FinanceBuzz found.
  • Parents who help adult children financially spend an average of $1,474 a month doing so, a Savings.com survey found.

What they are building

The trade publication Business of Home surveyed architects and designers working on these projects earlier this year. The features they described fall into three groups.

Places to sleep everyone. Bunk rooms are no longer only for small children. New Jersey designer Alma Russo, who specialises in beach houses for extended families, plans them for children and for college-age guests alike. Separate guest cottages, each with its own entrance, fridge and coffee bar, give adult couples their own space.

Reasons to stay. Pools, gyms, saunas and cold plunges, cinemas and wine cellars are common. New York architect Gil Schafer, who is working on six multigenerational properties, has added a bowling alley to one and an entertainment barn to another.

Ways to keep the peace. This is where the design gets practical. Atlanta architect Kevin Clark places screened porches away from bedrooms so late-night talkers do not wake sleepers. Builder Matt Kelly adds extra insulation between private rooms to stop sound travelling. Hard-wearing fabrics in patterns that hide stains are standard.

The scale has grown too. Clark said that 25 years ago a typical project was a three-acre site with one or two guest houses. His firm is now working on a compound of 300 acres.

Schafer adds a warning that others echo: however many amenities it has, the place “still has to feel like home”, and not like a resort.

Why now

The designers and the data point to four causes.

Housing is harder for the young. High prices, student debt and slow wage growth have kept many young adults from buying. Some wealthy clients told designers they worry their heirs will struggle to afford homes at all, and see a family property as security.

The pandemic changed habits. Lockdowns and remote work showed families that a house could be an office, a school and a holiday destination. Some now prefer to spend on a property over repeated holidays.

People are living longer. Parents in their sixties and seventies expect many active years with grandchildren and want room for them.

Land holds its value. A compound is seen as a legacy asset that can pass down the family, as well as a place to gather.

The other side of the story

The same forces look very different without wealth behind them.

Adult children returning home is common across America, and for most families it is a financial strain and not a design brief. The Savings.com survey found that parents supporting grown children spend $1,474 a month on average on things such as phone bills, health insurance and groceries. An AARP study found that 75% of older parents give their adult children money. Nearly half of parents in the Savings.com survey said they had sacrificed their own financial security to do so.

Financial advisers quoted in US coverage make the same point repeatedly: helping children at the cost of your own retirement can leave you dependent on them later.

A familiar idea for Indian families

What American designers call a new trend is ordinary life in much of India, where three generations under one roof is common and many families build a floor for each son or daughter.

The American experience still offers two useful ideas.

  • Design for privacy as well as togetherness. Separate entrances, sound insulation and a second kitchen are cheaper to plan at the start than to add later, and they are often what makes a shared home work.
  • Keep the paperwork clear. A property meant to stay in the family for generations needs clear ownership, a will and an agreed plan for who pays for upkeep. Family disputes over inherited property make up a large share of civil cases in Indian courts.

And one caution. A large home is an expensive asset to maintain and a hard one to sell quickly. It should be bought with money a family can spare, after retirement is provided for.
