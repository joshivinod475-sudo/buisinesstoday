IMF Chief Warns the World Economy Is 'Standing on One Leg' as AI Circular Financing Tops $1 Trillion

Kristalina Georgieva says a sudden loss of confidence in AI investment could threaten financial stability. Here is what the IMF found and what it means for India.

Editorial Desk Published 16 Nov 2026, 8:11 pm IST 4 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

The head of the International Monetary Fund has warned that the global economy has become dangerously dependent on a single source of growth: spending on artificial intelligence.

The global economy is “standing on one leg”, Kristalina Georgieva told CNBC on Monday, 16 November, on the sidelines of the ASEAN summit in the Philippines. “If this leg wobbles, that can lead to financial stability troubles.”

Her remarks came with a new figure. The IMF estimates that so-called circular financing among AI companies has passed $1 trillion, and that about two-thirds of it is funded with debt.

Key points

  • The IMF’s managing director says a sudden loss of confidence in AI investment is a risk to global growth.
  • Circular financing among AI companies is estimated at a little over $1 trillion, mostly borrowed.
  • Nvidia, at the centre of many of these deals, has lost more than $1 trillion in market value in a month.
  • Georgieva described recent market falls as “orderly” but said “we have to be watchful”.

What circular financing means

In a circular deal, a company invests in a customer, and the customer uses the money to buy the investor’s products. The investor then reports the purchase as revenue.

In the AI industry the pattern usually runs like this. A chipmaker or cloud company puts money into an AI developer. The developer spends it on computing power. That spending returns to the original investor as sales.

Each transaction is disclosed and legal. The concern is that the same money is being counted as investment by one company and as demand by another, which can make the industry look larger and healthier than the underlying customer spending would justify.

Georgieva’s own description of the total was measured: “not a huge size, but it is a rapid increase.”

Why it is a concern now

Three developments have come together.

The debt. According to the IMF’s estimate, about two-thirds of the circular financing is borrowed. Debt has to be repaid whether or not the AI products it paid for earn money.

The Nvidia crisis. Nvidia is the main supplier of AI chips and a party to many of the financing deals. Its chief executive resigned on 12 November, and the company has lost more than $1 trillion in market value over the past month, CNBC reported. UBS analyst Tim Arcuri estimated that a pullback in Nvidia’s financing could remove $50 billion or more from the system. We look at that in our report on Nvidia’s financing after Jensen Huang.

Rising interest rates. Borrowing costs for companies are going up, with the US Federal Reserve expected to raise rates in December.

How dependent the economy has become

The IMF raised its forecast for global growth in 2026 to 3.1% in October. Much of the support is coming from AI-related investment, at a time when an energy shock linked to the war with Iran is holding growth back elsewhere.

Trade tells the same story. The research firm Capital Economics has suggested that as much as 80% of the recent expansion in global exports is concentrated in AI-related goods, such as chips and servers.

In October the IMF published a worst-case scenario in which AI fails to meet expectations. In that case it projected a 20% fall in US share prices in 2026 and global output 1.1% below its baseline by 2028.

What Georgieva is not saying

She did not predict a crash. She called the market’s recent swings “orderly” and said they were unlikely to undermine the world economy.

Her argument is about expectations. Investor enthusiasm, she said, is running ahead of what the technology has so far proved it can do. “We tend to rush ahead,” she said, adding that “some corrections” in valuations are necessary.

That is a different claim from saying AI is a bubble. A technology can be transformative and still be overvalued for a time. The railways and the internet both changed the world, and both left many early investors with losses.

What it means for India

Markets. Indian shares are less exposed to AI than American ones, since India has few listed companies that make AI hardware. But foreign investors tend to sell emerging-market shares when US markets fall sharply, so a correction on Wall Street would be felt in Mumbai.

IT services. India’s large IT companies depend on technology budgets at American and European clients. If those clients cut spending after an AI downturn, order books would suffer.

The upside. India is a buyer of AI computing, not a seller. Cheaper chips and cloud capacity after a correction would lower costs for Indian companies building AI products.

For individual investors. The IMF’s warning is a reminder to check concentration. International and technology funds popular with Indian investors often hold the same handful of AI-linked American shares. A broad index fund spreads that risk.

What to watch this week

  • Nvidia’s quarterly results on 17 November, the first since its chief executive left.
  • The planned stock market listing of the AI developer Anthropic, a test of investor appetite.
  • The US Federal Reserve’s decision on 9 December.

This article is general information and not investment advice. It draws on Kristalina Georgieva’s interview with CNBC on 16 November 2026 and on the IMF’s October World Economic Outlook as reported there.
