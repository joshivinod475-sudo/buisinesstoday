Should AI Data Centres Pay More Tax? Trump's 'Dividend' Idea and Democrats' Plans Explained

Data centres enjoy big US tax breaks, and Democrats taking control of Congress want to cut them. Trump says data centres should pay a 'dividend' to communities. Here are the proposals and what they mean.

Editorial Desk Published 23 Nov 2026, 9:12 pm IST 4 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

The boom in AI data centres has become one of the biggest tax and political questions in Washington. Companies building them benefit from generous federal tax breaks, while local residents worry about higher electricity bills, water use and noise. After the November midterms, both parties are rethinking how much data centres should pay.

President Donald Trump has said companies building data centres should pay a “dividend” to the communities around them. Democrats, who take control of both chambers of Congress in January, have gone further, proposing to cut tax breaks or add new taxes.

Key points

  • Data centres benefit from tax breaks in the 2025 tax law, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, including full expensing of equipment.
  • Trump says data centres should “pay their own way” for power and pay a “dividend” to communities, but has not proposed a binding rule.
  • Senator Ron Wyden has proposed ending data centre tax incentives and imposing an ongoing tax on them.
  • A bill from Senator Mark Warner would deny bonus depreciation to AI data centres, which the Tax Foundation estimates would raise about $29.9 billion over 10 years.
  • A Congressional Research Service report on 18 November set out options including taxes on computing power and electricity use.

Why data centres pay less tax

Building an AI data centre costs billions of dollars, mostly for chips, servers and power equipment. Under current US law, companies can deduct much of that cost immediately rather than spreading it over many years. That sharply reduces their taxable profits in the early years.

Many states and local governments add their own incentives, such as sales tax exemptions on equipment and property tax breaks, to attract investment. Critics say these deals often create relatively few permanent jobs for the money involved.

The proposals

Proposal Who What it would do
Community “dividend” President Trump Companies pay communities hosting data centres. No amount or mechanism specified.
Ratepayer protection pledge White House and tech companies Voluntary commitment that data centres cover the cost of their power so households do not pay more
End incentives and tax data centres Sen. Ron Wyden (D) Remove tax breaks and add an ongoing federal tax on data centres
Deny bonus depreciation Sen. Mark Warner (D) Slower tax write-offs for AI data centres; about $29.9 billion over 10 years, according to the Tax Foundation
Exclude from Opportunity Zones Sen. Josh Hawley (R) Remove data centres from a tax programme meant for poorer areas

The Congressional Research Service, which advises lawmakers, has also listed options including a tax on AI “tokens”, a tax on computing power, a tax on digital advertising and a tax on electricity used by data centres.

The politics

Data centres were an issue in the midterm elections. Voters in several states approved measures that could slow construction, and Democratic candidates campaigned on rising power bills. We covered this in our report on Texas’s election and the data centre boom.

Trump has tried to balance support for the AI industry with voters’ concerns about costs. He has backed voluntary pledges and the idea of community payments but has avoided legally binding federal limits. Industry groups argue that new taxes would push investment to other countries, especially at a time when the US is competing with China.

What it means for AI companies and investors

Higher taxes or slower write-offs would raise the cost of building AI capacity at a time when spending is already enormous. The International Energy Agency expects data centre electricity demand to roughly double by 2030. Investors are already nervous: technology shares had their worst week since 2020 in the week of Anthropic’s market debut.

Any changes would need to pass a divided government, with a Democratic Congress and a Republican president, so a final outcome is likely to be a negotiated compromise.

Lessons for India

India is offering its own incentives to attract data centres, with states such as Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Telangana and Uttar Pradesh competing for investment. The US debate shows the questions India will face as capacity grows: who pays for new power lines, how water is shared, and whether tax breaks deliver enough local benefit.

Frequently asked questions

Do data centres pay taxes in the US? Yes, but federal tax breaks such as immediate expensing reduce their bills, and many states offer further exemptions.

Has Trump proposed a tax on data centres? No binding tax. He has said companies should pay a “dividend” to communities and cover their own power costs.

Will Congress tax data centres? Democrats, who control Congress from January, have proposed several measures, but any law would need the president’s signature.

This article is based on reporting by USA Today, Fox News, Bloomberg Tax and The Next Web, analysis by law firms Mayer Brown and Akin Gump, and a Congressional Research Service report. It is general information, not tax advice.
