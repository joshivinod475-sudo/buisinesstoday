Former SEC Chair Gary Gensler Says AI Is 'Already a Bubble' That Could Cause a Recession Before 2028

Gary Gensler told CNN the AI boom is a bubble likely to burst before the 2028 election, citing huge spending, circular financing and debt. Here is his case and the counter-arguments.

Editorial Desk Published 19 Nov 2026, 8:02 pm IST 3 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

The artificial intelligence boom is a bubble that is likely to burst and cause a US recession before the 2028 presidential election, former Securities and Exchange Commission chair Gary Gensler told CNN.

“It’s already a bubble,” said Gensler, who led the SEC from 2021 to 2025 and now teaches at MIT. “The only question is if it’s a soft recession or something more dramatic.”

His comments make him the most senior former US regulator to warn publicly of an AI-driven recession, CNN said.

Key points

  • Gensler pointed to AI infrastructure spending of as much as $800 billion a year by some estimates.
  • He said Big Tech companies are spending five times as much in 2026 as three years ago.
  • He warned that circular financing and off-balance-sheet debt make the risks hard to see.
  • Similar warnings have come from Ray Dalio, Harvard economist Kenneth Rogoff and investor Mike Novogratz.
  • The warning came days before Anthropic’s record IPO.

Gensler’s argument

History repeats. Transformative technologies, he said, often see a boom in infrastructure built ahead of the revenue it needs, followed by a bust. “That railroad boom-to-bubble was quite an adventure, and it led to really deep recessions, depressions. The 1920s with electricity, the 1990s with the internet,” he said.

Spending outruns profits. Money is pouring into data centres and chips faster than AI companies can turn it into profit. “It’s just the extraordinary size of it all,” he said.

Hidden borrowing. Gensler, who also chaired the Commodity Futures Trading Commission under President Obama, highlighted circular financing, where chipmakers invest in customers who then buy their chips, and complex off-balance-sheet structures. “I think history will show that they loaned too much money, too fast,” he said. The IMF has raised similar concerns about circular financing.

The counter-arguments

  • Real demand. Nvidia reported revenue up more than 100% and raised its forecast, and says customers are still short of computing capacity.
  • Productivity gains. US productivity rose 3.6% in the third quarter, a sign the investment may be starting to pay off.
  • Strong buyers. Much of the spending comes from very profitable companies such as Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta, which can afford it from cash flow.

A bubble can also last much longer than critics expect, and a correction in share prices need not mean a recession.

Why now

Gensler spoke at a fragile moment for AI. Democrats’ midterm wins raised the prospect of curbs on data centres in key states. Nvidia is still dealing with the fallout from Jensen Huang’s resignation. And Anthropic has just priced the largest IPO ever, a test of how much investors will pay for AI.

What it means for investors

  • Concentration risk. A handful of AI-linked companies make up a large share of US indices. Index investors are more exposed than they may realise.
  • Debt is the warning sign. Booms funded with equity hurt shareholders when they end. Booms funded with debt can hurt banks and the wider economy.
  • Diversify. No one can time a bubble. Spreading investments across sectors and countries reduces dependence on one theme.
