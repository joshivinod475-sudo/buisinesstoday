How Startups Raise Money: From Bootstrapping to Series A

Pre-seed, seed, angels, VCs, dilution: a founder-friendly map of the funding journey and what each stage expects.

By Editorial Desk 8 Nov 2026 2 min read

Startup funding news is full of jargon: pre-seed, Series A, valuation, dilution. Underneath, the logic is simple. At each stage, a startup sells a slice of ownership to investors in exchange for cash it needs to reach the next milestone.

Stage 0: Bootstrapping

Founders fund the company themselves, from savings, early customers or a side income. Nobody else owns a share yet. Bootstrapping forces discipline and keeps control, but growth is limited by the cash the business itself generates.

Stage 1: Friends, family and pre-seed

The first outside money often comes from people who trust the founder more than the idea. Amounts are small and the goal is to build a first version of the product and test whether anyone wants it.

Stage 2: Seed round and angel investors

Angel investors are individuals, often experienced founders or executives, who invest their own money. Seed funds are small venture firms that specialise in this stage. Here, investors usually want to see:

  • A real problem and a clear customer
  • An early product with some users or revenue
  • A team that can execute

Seed money pays for finding product-market fit, the point where customers clearly want what you sell.

Stage 3: Series A and beyond

Venture capital (VC) firms lead Series A rounds when a startup has a repeatable way of winning customers and wants to scale it. Investors look closely at numbers: revenue growth, customer retention and unit economics (does each customer earn more than it costs to acquire and serve?). Later rounds (Series B, C…) fund expansion into new markets and products.

Two terms every founder should understand

Valuation. If an investor puts in ₹2 crore for 20% of the company, the post-money valuation is ₹10 crore (₹2 crore ÷ 20%). The pre-money valuation, before the cash arrived, was ₹8 crore.

Dilution. Every round issues new shares, so existing owners’ percentages shrink. Owning 60% of a ₹10 crore company is worth more than 100% of a ₹1 crore company, if the money actually helps the company grow.

Alternatives to equity

Not every business should raise venture capital. Revenue-based financing, bank loans, government schemes (such as those under the Startup India initiative), grants and simply staying profitable are all valid paths, and none of them cost you ownership.

The takeaway

Raise money to reach a specific milestone, not because others are raising. Each round should buy enough time to make the company clearly more valuable before you need the next one.
