The Indian AI Start-ups Tackling Land Records, Potholes and Public Health

From Landeed's land-title search to pothole-spotting apps and Fractal's hospital tool, Indian AI start-ups are solving local problems. Here is what they do and what holds them back.

Editorial Desk Published 17 Nov 2026, 9:12 pm IST 4 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

India is not building the large AI models that dominate headlines. The United States and China lead there. Where Indian start-ups are finding their place is one step down the chain: applying AI to problems that are specific to India, often using messy, multilingual data and selling to customers who will not pay much.

A BBC report this week looked at several of them. Their stories show both the opportunity and the obstacles.

Key points

  • Landeed uses AI to pull together land records from 24 states and check property titles in minutes.
  • Apps that detect potholes from dash-cam video are being offered to city authorities.
  • Fractal Analytics, one of India’s few listed AI companies, runs a health tool in Mumbai’s municipal hospitals.
  • Funding for Indian AI start-ups rose 265% year on year, to more than $1 billion in the first nine months of 2026, according to the data platform Inc42.

Landeed: proving who owns a piece of land

In India, registering a sale deed does not prove that the seller owned the land. The system is called presumptive titling: the buyer must establish ownership by assembling other records, such as earlier deeds, tax receipts, survey maps and mutation entries, held by different offices.

Sanjay Mandava found this out in 2020, when he spent months collecting about a dozen records to settle a family land dispute in Chennai. He then founded Landeed.

The platform gathers records from state land revenue departments, registration offices, survey records and municipal databases, links each property to its survey number, and uses AI to read titles and flag inconsistencies. The company says it holds 1.8 billion documents, the largest repository of title data in the country, and is used by millions of people.

The problem matters because property disputes make up a large share of civil cases in Indian courts, and real estate holds most household wealth. Anyone buying property should still have a lawyer check the title. Tools like this make that check faster and cheaper, not unnecessary.

Potholes and public health

Roads. An app built by Bengaluru-based educator Gaurav Sen detects potholes using a dash-cam, satellite navigation and an image-recognition model, then identifies the officials responsible and sends them the data. At least half a dozen similar products exist. Sen says a city could fit the system to buses and taxis to map road conditions continuously.

Hospitals. Fractal Analytics has deployed a tool called Vaidya in hospitals run by Mumbai’s civic body. It gives patients simplified summaries of lab reports in several languages, helps them find suitable health facilities, gives doctors a summary before consultation, and gives administrators an anonymised dashboard of disease trends.

What holds them back

The founders the BBC spoke to described the same three problems.

Customers who cannot pay much. “The cost of building in India is sometimes much higher than what Indian customers are willing to pay,” said Srikanth Velamakanni, co-founder of Fractal. Computing power costs the same in India as in the US. The customers’ budgets do not.

Not enough chips. “I am starved for GPUs,” said Mandava. The government’s IndiaAI Mission has promised start-ups access to 38,000 GPUs, the processors used to train and run AI models, but demand is far greater.

Pilots that never become contracts. Government projects are a natural market for these tools, but turning a successful trial into a paid, recurring contract through public procurement is slow.

Experts quoted by the BBC warned that without progress on these, Indian companies risk building shallow products that the next wave of founders will easily replace.

What this means for founders and investors

  • Distribution beats technology. In public-sector markets, the hard part is procurement, not the model. Founders need people who understand government buying as much as engineers.
  • Data is the moat. Landeed’s advantage is 1.8 billion documents, not its algorithm. Proprietary, hard-to-collect data is what makes a product difficult to copy.
  • Price for India, or sell abroad. Many Indian AI firms will need overseas customers to cover their costs, as Indian IT services companies did before them.
  • Watch compute costs. GPU access is a real constraint on margins, and it is one reason the global AI investment cycle matters here. We looked at the risks in that cycle in our report on whether the AI boom is nearing its end.
