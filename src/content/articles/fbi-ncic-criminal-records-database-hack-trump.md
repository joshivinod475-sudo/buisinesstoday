Hackers have broken into a Federal Bureau of Investigation computer system used by police across the United States to share criminal justice information, President Donald Trump confirmed in an interview with Fox News on Saturday.

The intruders accessed the National Crime Information Center (NCIC), according to two people familiar with the matter cited by MS NOW. CNN first reported the suspected breach. It is not yet clear what data, if any, was taken.

Trump said the hack might have an upside. "Maybe we'll learn about the voter fraud, all the voter fraud that took place," he said. "I always like to put things on a positive spin."

## Key points

- The NCIC is the FBI's central database of wanted persons, missing persons, violent offenders, stolen property and related records.
- It is restricted to law enforcement but is not classified.
- The FBI did not immediately comment, and the scale of the breach has not been confirmed.
- It is the second known intrusion into FBI systems in recent months, after a breach of an FBI jobs website in September.
- Trump suggested the stolen data could reveal information about voter fraud and "Russia, Russia, Russia".

## What the NCIC is

The [National Crime Information Center](https://www.fbi.gov/services/cjis/ncic) has run since 1967 and is managed by the FBI's Criminal Justice Information Services division. Police officers, sheriffs, courts and federal agencies query it millions of times a day, often from patrol cars during a traffic stop.

According to the FBI and the people cited in reports, the system holds:

| Type of record | Examples |
|---|---|
| People | Wanted persons, missing persons, violent felons, sex offenders, people under protection orders |
| Identification | Fingerprint classifications, mugshots and identifying marks |
| Property | Stolen vehicles, firearms, licence plates and securities |
| Sensitive files | Known or suspected terrorists, foreign fugitives and gang members |

Because the records name victims, witnesses and missing children as well as suspects, a leak could expose people who have done nothing wrong.

## Why the breach matters

**Safety of individuals.** Protection-order and missing-person records can include addresses and details that, in the wrong hands, put vulnerable people at risk.

**Police operations.** If records were altered rather than just read, officers could act on false information. Agencies may need to verify entries before relying on them.

**Trust in data sharing.** About 18,000 US law enforcement agencies connect to FBI criminal justice systems. A breach at the centre raises questions about the security of every connection point, including local police networks that are often less well protected.

**Pattern of attacks.** In September, a group known as ShinyHunters broke into an FBI jobs website, exposing data on current and former employees and disrupting some agency operations until it was fixed. Two intrusions in quick succession will put pressure on the FBI and Congress over federal cybersecurity budgets.

## Trump's response

Trump's comments drew attention because the president usually leads the response to an attack on a federal system. "I wonder who's doing it, because wouldn't it be great to learn about Russia, Russia, Russia?" he said, referring to investigations of Russian interference in the 2016 election. "People should hear about it, because it actually put our country in danger, and so maybe we'll learn things that we wouldn't have learned otherwise."

There is no evidence that the NCIC contains information about voter fraud. It is a criminal records and wanted-persons database, not an elections system.

## Lessons for businesses and India

Government databases are among the most attractive targets for hackers because they combine sensitive personal data with wide access. India runs comparable systems, notably the [Crime and Criminal Tracking Network and Systems (CCTNS)](https://ncrb.gov.in/), which links more than 17,000 police stations. Such systems are only as secure as their weakest user account.

The same principles apply to companies holding customer data:

- **Limit access.** Give each user only the records they need.
- **Watch for unusual queries.** Bulk searches or downloads should trigger alerts.
- **Use strong authentication.** Stolen passwords remain the most common way in.

The breach also comes in a week of big moves in the security industry, including reports that [CrowdStrike is in talks to buy Zscaler](/tech/crowdstrike-zscaler-acquisition-talks/). For individuals, the advice is to watch for scams that use leaked data to sound official.
