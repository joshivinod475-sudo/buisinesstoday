Children living in Brazilian neighbourhoods where specially bred mosquitoes had been released were far less likely to catch dengue or chikungunya, researchers reported on Saturday. The results add strong evidence that the method, known as Wolbachia, can control mosquito-borne diseases alongside insecticides and bite prevention.

The four-year study followed more than 5,700 schoolchildren in Belo Horizonte, a dengue hotspot in Brazil. In neighbourhoods with Wolbachia-carrying mosquitoes, infections with dengue and chikungunya were about 69% lower than in similar neighbourhoods without them, said Dr Sri Edupuganti of Emory University, part of the international research team. The findings were presented at the annual meeting of the [American Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene](https://www.astmh.org/).

## Key points

- About 69% fewer dengue and chikungunya infections in children where Wolbachia mosquitoes were released.
- The effect held up during Brazil's severe dengue outbreak in 2024.
- Unlike earlier studies, researchers tested children's blood, catching mild infections that would otherwise go undiagnosed.
- There were too few Zika cases to measure any effect on Zika.
- The results come as Florida faces its largest local dengue outbreak in decades.

## How the Wolbachia method works

Wolbachia is a type of bacteria that is common in many insects and harmless to people. It does not naturally occur in *Aedes aegypti*, the mosquito that spreads dengue, chikungunya, Zika and yellow fever.

1. Scientists breed *Aedes aegypti* mosquitoes that carry Wolbachia.
2. The bacteria sharply reduce the mosquitoes' ability to pass on these viruses.
3. Infected females pass Wolbachia to their offspring.
4. Releasing enough of these mosquitoes, both males and females, lets Wolbachia spread through the local mosquito population over time.

Unlike spraying, which must be repeated, the effect is designed to be self-sustaining once Wolbachia becomes established. The non-profit [World Mosquito Program](https://www.worldmosquitoprogram.org/) developed the approach and has run releases in more than a dozen countries.

## Why this study matters

Earlier trials, notably a large randomised study in Yogyakarta, Indonesia, showed that Wolbachia releases reduced the number of dengue cases diagnosed in hospitals and clinics. But many people with dengue have mild symptoms or none, and are never tested.

The Brazilian study went further. Neighbourhoods were randomly assigned either to receive Wolbachia mosquitoes or not, while all received the same insecticide spraying and other standard controls. Researchers then tested the children's blood for signs of infection. That design makes it more likely that the drop was caused by the mosquitoes.

"This is really exciting," said Dr David Hamer, a global health expert at Boston University who was not involved in the study. He said combining Wolbachia with existing measures could "knock dengue down to much lower levels" in many places.

## Dengue is spreading

Dengue cases have risen sharply worldwide as warmer temperatures, urban growth and travel expand the range of *Aedes* mosquitoes. In the continental United States, dengue is usually found in travellers returning from abroad. As of Friday, however, Florida had recorded more than 650 locally acquired cases this year, about three-quarters of them in Hillsborough County, home to Tampa. It appears to be the largest outbreak in any US state since at least the 1940s.

## What it means for India

India has one of the world's highest dengue burdens. The [National Center for Vector Borne Diseases Control](https://ncvbdc.mohfw.gov.in/) records hundreds of thousands of cases in many years, and the true number is believed to be much higher.

India has already laid the groundwork. In 2022 the ICMR's Vector Control Research Centre in Puducherry, working with the World Mosquito Program, developed two lines of Wolbachia-carrying *Aedes aegypti* by crossing Australian strains with local Puducherry mosquitoes. Researchers said the lines were fit enough for possible pilot releases. We could not find public confirmation of large-scale field releases in India since then.

The Brazilian results strengthen the case for such releases in Indian cities, but they would need:

- **Public consent.** Releasing mosquitoes, even harmless ones, requires community trust.
- **Regulatory approval.** Biological control measures need clearance from health and biosafety authorities.
- **Sustained funding.** Releases must continue until Wolbachia is established in each area.

Wolbachia does not replace other measures. Removing standing water, using repellents and covering water containers remain essential, especially in the monsoon and post-monsoon months when dengue peaks.

## Economic cost

Dengue imposes a heavy economic cost through hospital bills, lost workdays and strain on public health systems. A method that cuts infections without repeated spraying could save money over time, though releases are costly to set up. Mosquito-borne disease is one of several areas where public health and economic productivity are closely linked, as with the [return of measles](/healthcare/mmr-booster-who-needs-extra-measles-shot/) in the United States.
