When Dr Raymond Kasereka Kamwanga told his family he thought he had Ebola and was going to a treatment centre, his mother begged him not to go. To many in his community, the centre was a place people went to die.

He went anyway, and survived. Now the 30-year-old doctor, who works at Katwa Hospital in Butembo in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo, is telling his story to persuade others to seek care early, the BBC reported.

## Key points

- The World Health Organization declared the outbreak a public health emergency of international concern in May.
- About 11,000 people have been infected and 5,400 have died, making it the second-deadliest Ebola outbreak recorded, according to the BBC.
- 3,031 people have survived.
- North Kivu province, where Butembo is, has the most cases.

## A survivor's account

Dr Kamwanga believes he caught the virus from a patient, in a setting where protective equipment was not always adequate. His first symptoms were mild: a bitter taste, headaches and a general feeling of being unwell.

Butembo cannot test for Ebola quickly. Samples are sent to Beni, 54km away, and poor roads in the conflict-hit region mean the journey can take two to three days. By his fourth day at home he was very weak, vomiting and had diarrhoea.

He walked to the treatment centre that evening, and called ahead so that colleagues could put on protective gear before they approached him. He is now writing a book about the experience. "I am living proof that the disease exists, but also that it is possible to recover from it," he told the BBC.

## What Ebola is

Ebola is a severe and often fatal disease caused by a virus. It is not spread through the air like flu.

- **How it spreads:** through direct contact with the blood, vomit, faeces or other body fluids of a sick person or someone who has died of the disease, or with contaminated objects such as bedding.
- **Symptoms:** fever, tiredness, muscle pain, headache and sore throat, followed by vomiting, diarrhoea and, in some cases, bleeding.
- **Incubation:** symptoms appear between 2 and 21 days after infection. A person is not infectious until symptoms begin.

## Why early treatment matters

Survival depends heavily on how early a patient receives care. Treatment centres replace lost fluids, manage symptoms and, for the Zaire species of the virus, can give approved antibody treatments that work best when given early.

Fear works against this. Families who see few people return from treatment centres keep patients at home, which both lowers the patient's chance of survival and exposes relatives and carers. Survivors who speak publicly, as Dr Kamwanga does, are among the most effective ways to break that cycle.

Vaccination of contacts and health workers, safe burials and tracing everyone who has been near a patient are the other pillars of control.

## Why it matters beyond Congo

**Health workers.** Doctors and nurses are among the most exposed. Protective equipment and training save lives on both sides of the bed.

**Conflict and roads.** Eastern Congo has suffered years of armed conflict. Insecurity and poor infrastructure slow testing and make it harder for teams to reach patients, which is why outbreaks there last longer.

**Global risk.** Outbreaks that are not contained can spread across borders through travel. Most countries, including India, screen travellers from affected regions during declared emergencies.

**Trust.** The same lesson appears in other outbreaks, such as the return of measles in the United States, which we covered in our report on [the US losing its measles elimination status](/healthcare/us-loses-measles-elimination-status/): disease control depends on whether people trust health services enough to use them.

## For travellers

Anyone travelling to affected parts of DR Congo or neighbouring countries should follow official health advice, avoid contact with sick people and with dead bodies, and seek care immediately if they develop a fever within 21 days of returning, telling the doctor where they have been.
