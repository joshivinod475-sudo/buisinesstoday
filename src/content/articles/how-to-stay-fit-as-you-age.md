The body changes with age. Muscle shrinks, balance becomes less sure and stamina falls. Much of that decline, though, comes from doing less and not from getting older.

That is the consistent finding behind official health guidance, and it is good news, because inactivity can be changed at any age. The same point was made this month by the 80-year-olds who completed the first [Super Age Games](/healthcare/super-age-games-longevity-competition/) in New York, one of whom only began training in his seventies.

Here is what the guidelines recommend, and how to put them into a week.

## Key points

- Adults aged 65 and over are advised to do at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week.
- Strength training on at least two days a week is part of the recommendation.
- Balance exercises are advised to reduce the risk of falls.
- Some activity is better than none, and benefits increase with more.

## What the guidelines say

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention sets out [three targets for adults aged 65 and over](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/older-adults.html). The World Health Organization's guidance is closely similar.

| Type | How much | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Aerobic | At least 150 minutes a week at moderate intensity, or 75 minutes at vigorous intensity | Brisk walking, cycling, swimming; jogging counts as vigorous |
| Muscle-strengthening | At least 2 days a week, working all major muscle groups | Weights, resistance bands, body-weight exercises |
| Balance | Regularly | Walking heel to toe, standing up from a chair |

The 150 minutes can be split up. Thirty minutes on five days is the usual suggestion, and shorter sessions add up.

Moderate intensity means your breathing and heart rate rise but you can still talk. If you can sing, speed up. If you cannot speak a sentence, you have reached vigorous effort.

The CDC adds a line that matters for anyone who finds the targets daunting: "some physical activity is better than none at all". People who cannot meet them because of a health condition are advised to be "as physically active as your abilities and conditions allow".

## Why each part matters

**Stamina** protects the heart, helps control blood pressure and blood sugar, and is linked to better mood and sleep.

**Strength** is the part most often skipped, and arguably the most important after 60. Muscle is lost steadily with age. Keeping it is what allows a person to climb stairs, carry shopping and get up off the floor.

**Balance** is about falls. A fall in later life can mean a broken hip, a long hospital stay and a permanent loss of independence. Balance improves with practice like any other skill.

## A week that meets the guidelines

This is one way to fit it together. Adjust it to your level.

- **Monday:** 30-minute brisk walk. Five minutes of balance practice.
- **Tuesday:** Strength session, 20 to 30 minutes.
- **Wednesday:** 30-minute walk, cycle or swim.
- **Thursday:** Rest, or gentle stretching or yoga.
- **Friday:** Strength session. Balance practice.
- **Saturday:** 30 to 40 minutes of something you enjoy: a longer walk, gardening, dancing, a game.
- **Sunday:** 30-minute walk.

## Strength exercises that need no equipment

Aim for 8 to 12 repetitions of each, and two rounds once that feels comfortable.

1. **Sit to stand.** Rise from a chair without using your hands, then lower yourself slowly.
2. **Wall press-ups.** Hands on a wall at shoulder height, bend the elbows and push back.
3. **Heel raises.** Hold a chair back and rise on to your toes.
4. **Step-ups.** Step on to the bottom stair and down, leading with each leg in turn.
5. **Carrying.** Walk across a room holding a filled water bottle or a bag in each hand.

## Balance practice

- Stand on one leg near a wall or counter, building up to 30 seconds each side.
- Walk in a straight line placing the heel of one foot directly in front of the toes of the other.
- Yoga and tai chi both train balance and are widely taught.

## Starting late, or after a long break

- **Begin below what you think you can do** and add a little each week. Ten minutes a day is a real start.
- **See a doctor first** if you have heart disease, diabetes, high blood pressure, joint problems, dizziness, or have not exercised for years.
- **Expect mild muscle soreness.** Sharp pain, chest pain, breathlessness at rest or dizziness are reasons to stop and seek advice.
- **Make it social.** People who exercise with others are more likely to keep going.
- **Protect your joints.** Swimming, cycling and walking on level ground are easier on knees than running.

## Food and rest

Exercise is half of it. Muscle needs protein to rebuild, and older adults often eat too little of it. Dal, curd, paneer, eggs, fish and pulses are all good sources, and it helps to include some at every meal. People with kidney disease should ask their doctor before increasing protein.

Sleep and water matter too. Thirst becomes a less reliable signal with age, so it is worth drinking regularly, especially in hot weather.

## For readers in India

- **The morning walk is a strong base.** Most walkers already meet the stamina target. What is usually missing is strength and balance work.
- **Yoga covers balance and flexibility well.** Add some strengthening, since most gentle yoga does not load the muscles enough.
- **Mind the heat and the air.** Walk early in summer, and choose indoor exercise on days when air pollution is high.
- **Use what is nearby.** Many city parks now have open-air gym equipment, and stairs are free.
- **Think of it as an investment.** Staying mobile reduces the risk of the falls and chronic illnesses that drive the largest health costs in later life. No insurance policy pays out as reliably.

## The bottom line

The guidance is simple: move on most days, strengthen your muscles twice a week and practise your balance. Starting at 70 is worth doing. Starting at 50 is better, and the best plan is one you will still be following a year from now.

*This article is general information and not medical advice. Speak to a doctor before starting a new exercise programme, particularly if you have a health condition. It draws on guidance from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the World Health Organization.*
