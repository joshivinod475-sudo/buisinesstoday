The Democratic upset in the Texas governor's race, and wins for the party in several other states, could slow the biggest investment boom in modern US history: the construction of data centres for artificial intelligence.

From January, states led by Democrats who support extending or expanding moratoriums on data centre construction will account for 45% of America's planned data centre capacity, according to Goldman Sachs estimates cited by CNN. A quarter of the planned US AI infrastructure build-out is in Texas alone.

## Key points

- Democrats gained governorships in Texas, Nevada, Georgia and Ohio, after campaigns that included tighter data centre rules.
- Proposals include ending tax breaks, stricter electricity and climate rules and letting local governments impose their own moratoriums.
- Wells Fargo estimates that every 10% cut in planned Texas capacity would reduce Big Tech's AI infrastructure spending by about 1.6%.
- AI investment accounted for as much as a quarter of US economic growth this year, according to ING economists.

## Why data centres became an election issue

Data centres bring construction jobs and tax revenue, but they also use large amounts of electricity and water. In many communities, residents have blamed them for rising power bills, strain on the grid and noise. That made them a campaign issue in states with the most projects.

In Texas, Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa could revisit state permitting rules and guarantees of electricity service for big new users, according to legal experts quoted by CNN. Texas law protects some existing local permits, but not against changes at state level.

## How much spending is at stake

AI infrastructure spending is expected to approach $1 trillion a year for the foreseeable future, according to Columbia Business School economist Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh. As a share of GDP, that would exceed every previous US investment boom, including the railways.

Hundreds of projects still waiting for permits, power connections or tax incentives are most exposed, said Mona Dajani, a lawyer at Cooley who works on infrastructure deals. Delays are costly: interest, equipment commitments and utility charges keep accruing while a project waits.

## Why a slowdown is not a collapse

- **Demand for AI is strong.** Companies continue to report shortages of computing capacity.
- **Federal power.** President Trump, who backs the build-out, can veto federal legislation, though states control much of the permitting.
- **Limits on governors.** Many projects are already under way or protected, and governors cannot block all of them.

Some economists see a silver lining. A slower build-out could ease the inflation pressure from AI spending, which the [Federal Reserve now flags as a risk](/markets/treasury-yields-fed-rate-hike-december-ai-inflation/), and weed out weaker projects financed with heavy debt.

## The wider picture

The election result adds to a list of pressures on the AI trade, from the [leadership turmoil at Nvidia](/markets/nvidia-q3-earnings-record-revenue-after-huang/) to warnings of a bubble. Grid limits are a constraint everywhere: the [IEA warns that data centre power use could double by 2030](/world/data-centre-electricity-demand-iea-2030-grids/) and that connections are already backed up.

For India, a slower US build-out could make other locations, including Indian data centre hubs, more attractive to global companies looking for power and land, though India faces its own grid and water constraints.

*This article is based on reporting by CNN and estimates by Goldman Sachs, Wells Fargo and ING cited there. Policy proposals by governors-elect may change before they take office.*
