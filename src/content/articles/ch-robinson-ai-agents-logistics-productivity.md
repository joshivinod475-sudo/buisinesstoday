Most companies experimenting with artificial intelligence are still running pilots. C.H. Robinson, one of the world's largest freight brokers, says it is past that stage.

The 120-year-old American company says it has put "hundreds" of AI agents to work across its operations and that productivity has risen by 65% as a result. It set out the case this week in an article it paid to place on the technology site TechCrunch.

Paid articles deserve careful reading, so we checked the claims against the company's published financial results. Some are supported by its filings. Others are the company's own figures and cannot be verified from outside.

## Key points

- C.H. Robinson arranges about 37 million shipments a year and says AI agents now handle much of the routine work.
- Its third-quarter results confirm rising productivity: revenue grew while average headcount fell 11.4% from a year earlier.
- Several headline figures, including time savings and customer benefits, come only from the company.
- The lesson it draws is that AI is only as good as the data and business context behind it.

## What an "AI agent" does here

An AI agent is software that can carry out a task with several steps, not just answer a question. C.H. Robinson's approach is to build many small agents, each with a narrow job, and link them together.

The company's examples:

- **Reading orders sent by email.** Agents extract the shipment details, interpret the customer's instructions, check the data, fill gaps and start the booking. The company says this used to take about four hours and now takes about 90 seconds.
- **Pricing.** It says every quote request is now answered through AI-powered workflows.
- **Managing shipments.** For customers of its managed service, it says 92% of shipments are handled autonomously.

Two further systems sit above the agents: one that coordinates work between them, and one that studies the results to find improvements. The company calls its overall programme "Lean AI".

## What the financial results confirm

C.H. Robinson reported results for the quarter to 30 September on 28 October. These are the company's official figures, filed with the US securities regulator.

| Measure | Third quarter of 2026 |
|---|---|
| Revenue | About $5.36 billion, up 29.5% from a year earlier |
| Adjusted earnings per share | $1.80, up 28.6% |
| Average headcount | Down 11.4% from a year earlier |
| Productivity in its two main divisions | Up more than 15% from a year earlier |
| Cumulative productivity gain since the end of 2022 | More than 65% |
| North American truckload volume | Up about 3.5%, against a 0.3% fall in a widely used freight index |

So the central claim holds up in broad terms. The company is handling more freight with fewer people, and has been gaining share in a weak market.

Productivity here is measured as shipments handled per person per day. It is a real measure, and it is also one that rises automatically when staff numbers fall.

## What rests on the company's word

The sponsored article contains figures that do not appear in the financial statements and come with no explanation of how they were calculated:

- hundreds of hours of routine work saved each day
- for customers, up to 23% faster speed to market, up to 35% better on-time pickup and "25% potential delivered savings"
- more than 100 trillion data points gathered from its shipments

Phrases such as "up to" and "potential" describe best cases. They may well be accurate. A reader cannot check them.

## The cost in jobs

A fall of 11.4% in average headcount in one year is large. The company presents it as productivity. For the people concerned it is fewer jobs, mainly in the routine, process-heavy roles that agents have taken over.

This is the trade-off that every company adopting AI at scale will face, and it is rarely stated in promotional material. Investors have welcomed it. The effect on service when things go wrong, and automated systems meet a situation they were not built for, takes longer to show.

## The lesson C.H. Robinson draws

"AI itself is not a differentiator," Arun Rajan, the company's chief strategy and innovation officer, says in the article. "It's only as effective as the data and context and discipline that power it."

His point is that the same AI models are available to everyone. What a competitor cannot easily copy is decades of shipment records and the knowledge of how freight actually moves: which carrier a customer prefers, which warehouse closes early, which route floods in the monsoon.

Stripped of the sales pitch, the advice is sound and applies well beyond logistics:

1. **Start with one narrow, repetitive task**, not a company-wide programme.
2. **Fix the data first.** An agent working from incomplete records makes confident mistakes.
3. **Measure one thing that matters**, and measure it the same way every quarter.
4. **Keep people in the loop** for exceptions and for anything a customer would be upset to have handled by a machine.

## Why this matters for Indian business

Logistics costs in India are high by international standards, and the sector still runs largely on phone calls, messaging apps and paper. That makes it fertile ground for the same kind of automation, and several Indian freight and trucking platforms are building it.

The caution is the one in Rajan's own remark. Automation rewards companies that already hold clean, detailed records. A firm that digitises its bookings, tracking and billing properly is preparing for AI whether or not it buys any. One that bolts an agent on to messy data will mostly automate its errors.
