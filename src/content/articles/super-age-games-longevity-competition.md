Sport has long had contests for the fastest and the strongest. A new one asks a different question: how well is your body ageing?

The first Super Age Games were held on 7 November at the Armory track in New York. Competitors aged from their twenties to their eighties went through the same set of physical trials and left with a number meant to show how their body is functioning compared with a typical person of their age.

The event is part of a fast-growing longevity industry. It is also an appealing idea with a real question hanging over it: what does the score actually tell you?

## Key points

- Competitors completed eight trials covering balance, agility, strength and aerobic fitness, in the style of mass-participation fitness races.
- Each received a "Super Age" number, combining the trial results with a questionnaire about lifestyle.
- The field ranged in age from 21 to 81, according to the organisers.
- Whether the score predicts future health has not been established.

## How the Games work

The format borrows from Hyrox, the indoor fitness race in which everyone completes the same circuit of exercises. Here the stations are chosen for what they reveal about ageing: staying upright on one leg, changing direction quickly, lifting and carrying, and sustaining effort.

The results feed into a single figure, the Super Age. If a 60-year-old performs like a typical 50-year-old, the number is meant to reflect it. Organisers say the event was designed with the Buck Institute for Research on Aging, a California research centre.

How many took part depends on who is counting. The organisers announced 960 "founding athletes" aged 20 to 80. Results posted afterwards showed about 370 competitors, one report said, and another put the field at about 500. A company statement described participants from 14 countries and 40 US states.

## The 80-year-olds

The competitors who drew most attention were the oldest. USA Today profiled two 80-year-olds who completed the trials.

One, a woman from the state of Georgia, has been active all her life and hiked the Appalachian Trail alone at 71. She told the newspaper she wants to live to 100 and intends to come back fitter next year.

The other, a retired car dealer from California, described himself as badly out of shape until his seventies. He spent about ten years building his fitness and said he was encouraged by the idea that it is possible to start at any age.

Between them they make the two points that researchers on ageing stress most: activity sustained over a lifetime pays off, and starting late still helps.

## What the trials measure, and why those

The choice of tests reflects established science, even if the scoring is new. Simple measures of physical function are among the better predictors of how people fare in later life.

- **Balance.** Poor balance raises the risk of falls, a leading cause of injury and loss of independence in older people.
- **Strength.** Muscle is lost with age unless it is used. Grip and leg strength are linked in many studies to disability and survival.
- **Aerobic fitness.** The capacity of the heart and lungs is one of the strongest known markers of long-term health.
- **Agility and speed.** How fast someone walks or gets up from a chair is widely used by doctors as a quick check on overall function.

## The caution about the score

A single number is satisfying. It should be treated with care.

The Super Age figure is a new formula. One report noted that its value as a predictor of future health has not been established. A good score on one day says how you performed in those eight tests. It does not promise a long life, and a poor score does not predict a short one.

There is also a commercial side. Longevity has become a large market for supplements, tests, clinics and events, and scores that can be improved are an effective way to sell them. Most of what is known to help costs very little.

## What you can do without a competition

The lesson of the Games does not require an entry fee.

1. **Move most days.** Public health guidance for older adults is at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, such as brisk walking.
2. **Train strength twice a week.** Body-weight exercises count.
3. **Practise balance.** Standing on one leg while brushing your teeth is a start.
4. **Test yourself simply.** Can you rise from a chair without using your hands? Walk briskly for ten minutes? Track those over time.
5. **See a doctor before a big change** if you have a heart condition, diabetes or joint problems.

## Why this matters in India

India's population is ageing quickly. The number of people over 60 is projected to roughly double by 2050, and with it the burden of diabetes, heart disease and falls.

Organised fitness for older adults is still uncommon in India outside a few cities, and many people stop exercising in their fifties on the assumption that age requires rest. The evidence points the other way. The parks full of morning walkers in Indian cities are the right idea. What is usually missing is strength and balance work, which matter most for staying independent.

For families, it is also a financial question. Keeping an older relative mobile is far cheaper than treating the consequences of a fall.
