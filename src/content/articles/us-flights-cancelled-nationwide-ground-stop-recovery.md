Air travel across the United States is still recovering after the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) halted every departure in the country on Friday, 13 November.

The order, known as a nationwide ground stop, lasted a little more than four hours. Its effects have lasted days. Airlines cancelled thousands of flights on Friday and Saturday and were still cancelling on Sunday as they moved aircraft and crews back to where they needed to be.

## Key points

- The FAA stopped all departures on Friday afternoon, US time, and lifted the order at 8.15 pm Eastern time.
- It was the first nationwide ground stop since January 2023 and only the second since the attacks of 11 September 2001.
- Officials blame a failure in the air traffic control communications network during an equipment upgrade. They say there is no sign of a cyberattack.
- The FAA expected schedules to be back to normal by the end of Sunday, with full capacity on Monday.

## The timeline

**Friday, 13 November.** Air traffic controllers began losing reliable radio and radar contact with aircraft. The FAA ordered all departures held. More than 3,000 flights already in the air landed safely. The stop was lifted at 8.15 pm Eastern time, after more than four hours. The Associated Press described it as the longest such halt in 25 years.

**Saturday, 14 November.** Airlines cancelled thousands more flights. Passengers slept in terminals or looked for hire cars, buses and trains. Several sports fixtures were rescheduled.

**Sunday, 15 November.** Cancellations fell to about 1,500 as operations recovered. FAA administrator Bryan Bedford said the "worst is behind us".

## Update, 16 November: the final count

About 18,000 US flights were cancelled between Friday and Monday morning, according to the tracking service FlightAware, CNBC reported. Hundreds of thousands of travellers were stranded. Bad weather on Thursday and Friday added to the disruption.

By Monday the system was close to normal, with nearly 300 cancellations and about 1,000 delays, a fraction of Sunday's figures.

The aviation data firm Cirium counted the cancellations by airline:

| Airline | Flights cancelled | Share of schedule |
|---|---|---|
| American | 5,367 | About 22% |
| Delta | 3,434 | About 17% |
| Southwest | Not given | More than 16% |
| United | Not given | More than 16% |

Airlines waived change fees and fare differences and offered extra pay to pilots and cabin crew to get aircraft moving. The cause is still under investigation. Two routers, from facilities in Arkansas and Texas, are being examined at a laboratory run by the contractor L3Harris.

## How many flights were affected

The counts differ by source and by what is being measured.

| Day | Cancellations | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Friday | About 12,460, including about 9,080 by major airlines | FAA, as reported by US media |
| Saturday | More than 5,100, rising to about 6,500 during the day | FlightAware and ABC News |
| Sunday | About 1,500 | US media reports |

The US normally handles about 45,000 flights a day. One aviation consultant quoted by AP estimated that Friday's stop affected about a quarter of them.

## What caused it

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the contractor L3Harris Technologies was replacing old copper communication lines with fibre-optic cable when the system became overloaded.

According to his account, a router was switched on at a facility in El Dorado, Arkansas, after new cable was installed. A problem then appeared at another facility in Amarillo, Texas, and spread through the network. The FAA said this caused "intermittent" communications and gaps in radar coverage.

Two routers have been removed for testing. L3Harris said the routers "did not fail" and that it is supporting the FAA's investigation. Officials have said they are examining whether a faulty router or an error by the contractor was responsible. That question is unresolved.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there was "zero evidence" of a cyberattack, though he would not rule one out entirely. Microsoft, which had [a global outage of its own the day before](/tech/microsoft-365-global-outage-restored/), said it had no indication the two events were connected.

## Why it takes days to recover

A four-hour stop does not end when departures resume. Aircraft and crews finish the day in the wrong cities, and pilots and cabin crew reach the legal limits on how long they may work. Our explainer on [why a fault on the ground disrupts flights thousands of kilometres away](/world/why-ground-stop-disrupts-flights-far-away/) covers this in detail.

The timing matters. The Thanksgiving holiday on 26 November is the busiest travel period of the American year. Duffy said he expected the system to be fully recovered before then.

## What airlines are doing

American Airlines capped fares on its domestic and short international routes and waived change fees for travel through the middle of the week, US media reported. Other carriers issued similar waivers allowing passengers to rebook without charge.

## If you are travelling to or within the US

- **Check your flight before leaving for the airport.** Use the airline's app, which usually updates before airport screens do.
- **Know your refund rights.** Under US rules, a passenger whose flight is cancelled and who chooses not to travel is entitled to a refund, including on a non-refundable ticket. Accepting a rebooking is optional.
- **Rebook online.** It is usually faster than queuing at a desk or calling.
- **Leave time for connections.** Travellers from India connecting through US hubs such as New York, Chicago or San Francisco should allow longer between flights this week.
- **Keep receipts.** Travel insurance may cover hotel and meal costs. Check the policy wording.

## The bigger argument

The failure has reopened a political fight over paying for a new air traffic control system. President Trump has asked Congress for more money, and Duffy has put the need at about $30 billion on top of the $12.5 billion approved last year.
