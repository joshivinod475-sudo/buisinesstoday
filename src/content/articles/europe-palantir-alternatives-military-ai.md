European armed forces are trying to reduce their reliance on American software for some of their most sensitive work, and one company is at the centre of the effort: Palantir.

The US firm's data and artificial intelligence platforms are used by the Pentagon, by Ukraine and by NATO. Several European governments now want an alternative of their own. The difficulty, Reuters reported on Monday, is that nothing built in Europe yet matches it.

## Key points

- Germany has drawn up a shortlist for a military data platform that does not include Palantir.
- France is building a system called Arcadia and plans a field-ready version by mid-2027.
- The Netherlands wants a European alternative within two years.
- Officials admit the European options are less mature. One German source called them "good enough".

## What this software does

Modern armies collect enormous amounts of information: satellite images, drone video, radio intercepts, sensor readings and reports from soldiers. Software of the kind Palantir sells pulls these together on one screen, uses AI to highlight what matters and helps commanders decide what to do.

Palantir's Maven Smart System is the Pentagon's core platform for this work and is widely used by Ukraine. NATO's military headquarters adopted it last year and said in June that it was ready for routine use across the alliance.

"I've yet to find anything that can pull together as much of a diverse set of data," NATO's top commander, US Air Force General Alexus Grynkewich, told Reuters.

## What each country is doing

| Country | Plan |
|---|---|
| Germany | Aims to deploy an AI-based military data platform from next year. Its shortlist excludes Palantir. The German firm Helsing has launched a product called Clarity and says it could supply a first version within months |
| France | Developing a sovereign platform, Arcadia, with several suppliers. Some contracts are due by the end of the year, with a version ready for use in the field by mid-2027 |
| Netherlands | Uses some Palantir software now. Wants a European alternative within two years, probably combining Dutch and other European suppliers |

Other European suppliers include Denmark's Systematic, whose SitaWare software is used by the German army and parts of NATO, the French groups Thales and Safran, and the Paris startup Comand AI.

## Why Europe wants its own

**Washington is stepping back.** The Trump administration has pressed Europe to take on more of its own defence as the US reduces its military presence on the continent. Relying on an American company for the software that runs a battle sits uneasily with that.

**Fear of being cut off.** European officials worry about depending on a system that a foreign supplier, or its government, could restrict. Palantir has tried to reassure them that the system NATO uses has no "kill switch".

**A precedent in space.** Satellite communications raise the same issue. The sector is dominated by SpaceX, and Ukraine reported further significant disruption to its access last week.

**Data.** Governments want to control who trains AI systems on their military data and where that data is kept.

## What stands in the way

**A capability gap.** Officials told Reuters that Europe has no direct equivalent to Palantir's scale and experience in real operations. A source said Germany's shortlisted options would not match it but would be "good enough", would keep data under national control, and could meet about 80% of the need within a year.

**Fragmentation.** Europe has many small suppliers and many national programmes. "It's very fragmented, and that is the problem," said Derk Boswijk, the Dutch minister responsible for arms procurement.

**Working together.** Allies must be able to share information in a war. "There's the issue of interoperability when we fight in alliances," said Lieutenant General Christian Freuding, the head of the German army. If NATO uses one system and member states use several others, something has to connect them.

**Time.** NATO officials have warned that Russia could be in a position to attack allied territory as early as 2029, a claim Moscow denies. A system that is nearly as good and available soon may be worth more than a perfect one that arrives late.

## Palantir's response

The company says demand in Europe continues. Louis Mosley, who runs its international business, pointed to the United Kingdom, where the defence ministry signed a three-year agreement last year. He told Reuters he still hopes to persuade Germany, on the argument that the software has been tested in combat and works today.

## The business angle

For investors, this is a test of how far political pressure can shift a market that technology alone would not.

- **European defence technology firms** stand to gain orders, and several have raised large sums from investors on that expectation.
- **Palantir** risks losing some European contracts while keeping its position with NATO and the UK.
- **The trade-off is real.** Governments are choosing to accept a weaker product for a time in return for control. That is a cost, and taxpayers will bear it.

## Why this matters for India

India knows this argument well. It has pursued self-reliance in defence equipment for years, for much the same reasons: the risk that a foreign supplier could withhold spare parts, software updates or support in a crisis.

Two lessons carry over.

- **Software is now part of that question.** Sovereignty over aircraft and missiles means little if the systems that direct them depend on a foreign licence.
- **There is a market here.** Indian IT and defence electronics companies have the engineering skills this kind of software needs. Countries that want an alternative to both American and Chinese systems may prove willing buyers.
