Measles is back in the United States. This week, the US, Mexico and Bolivia lost their measles elimination status, meaning the virus has spread continuously in each country for at least 12 months. More than 5,400 cases have been reported across 45 US states this year, and 10 people have died, as of 17 November.

That has many adults asking whether they need another shot. For most people, doctors say the answer is no. "Most people are protected without getting a booster shot," Dr Richard Martinello of Yale School of Medicine told the Associated Press.

## Key points

- Two doses of the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine usually protect for life. So does having had measles.
- One dose prevents measles 93% of the time. Two doses raise that to 97%.
- Some groups should consider an extra dose, including people vaccinated between 1963 and 1967.
- Babies aged 6 to 11 months who are travelling or near an outbreak can get an early "zero dose".
- A zero dose does not replace the two routine doses.

## Who may need another dose

| Group | Why |
|---|---|
| People vaccinated from 1963 to 1967 | Some received an early, less effective vaccine made with killed virus. Anyone who did, or is unsure, should get at least one dose of today's vaccine. |
| Healthcare workers born before 1957 without proof of immunity | People born before 1957 are generally considered immune, but health workers face higher exposure. |
| Students, especially in colleges and dormitories | Close living conditions help measles spread. |
| International travellers | Measles is common in many countries. |
| People living with someone whose immune system is weak | To protect a household member who cannot be vaccinated. |

If you cannot find your vaccination records, try your parents, your doctor, past schools or employers, or your local health department. A blood test can show whether you have measles antibodies, but Martinello said the result does not map perfectly to how well protected you are. Another dose is safe for most people. It is not given during pregnancy.

## The "zero dose" for babies

Babies are especially vulnerable to measles, because the antibodies they receive from their mothers fade during the first months of life.

In the US, routine MMR shots start at 12 to 15 months. Experts have long advised an early "zero dose" for babies aged 6 to 11 months who are travelling abroad or living near an outbreak. A new review by the University of Minnesota's [Center for Infectious Disease Research and Policy (CIDRAP)](https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/) found the early dose is safe and effective:

- **Before 9 months:** about 58% effective
- **From 9 to 11 months:** about 83% effective

Babies who receive a zero dose still need the usual two doses later, because protection from an early shot may not last.

The vaccine can cause fever, which in some children leads to febrile seizures. These are frightening but usually harmless. The review's co-author, Dr Heidi Moline, said the risk is "very low" and found no evidence that it is higher in younger babies. "As we travel and gather for the holidays, parents of infants are encouraged to talk to their child's health provider to see if a zero dose makes sense," she said.

## How India's schedule differs

India vaccinates earlier than the US because measles has circulated widely for longer. Under the government's [Universal Immunisation Programme](https://nhm.gov.in/index1.php?lang=1&level=2&sublinkid=824&lid=220), children receive the measles–rubella (MR) vaccine free:

- **First dose:** at 9 to 12 months
- **Second dose:** at 16 to 24 months

Private paediatricians often use the MMR vaccine, which also protects against mumps, on a similar schedule. India has set a target to eliminate measles and rubella, and has run large catch-up campaigns. Parents should follow their paediatrician's advice and keep the vaccination card safe, since it is the easiest proof of immunity later in life.

## Travelling to the US or Canada?

With holiday travel ahead, measles exposure is more likely in airports and in communities with outbreaks. Before travelling:

- Check that every family member has had two doses of a measles-containing vaccine.
- Ask your doctor about an early dose for babies aged 6 months or older.
- Carry vaccination records in case they are needed.

## Why it matters for the economy

Measles outbreaks are expensive. Each case triggers contact tracing, quarantines, missed school and work, and hospital stays for serious cases. US health departments have spent heavily on outbreak responses this year. Vaccination remains one of the most cost-effective public health measures available.
