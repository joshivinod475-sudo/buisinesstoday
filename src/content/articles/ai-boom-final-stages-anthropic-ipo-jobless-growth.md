The artificial intelligence company Anthropic is due to price its initial public offering on Wednesday and start trading on Thursday. It would be the first of the leading AI developers to list its shares, and one of the largest share sales on record.

It arrives at an awkward moment. Economists are warning that the stock market's AI boom is in its late stages. The US economy is growing, but it is creating few jobs. And Nvidia, the company at the centre of the boom, lost its chief executive this month.

## Update, 18 November: Anthropic expected to price above its range

Anthropic is likely to price its shares above the marketed range of $70 to $78, multiple people told CNBC on Wednesday morning. The research firm New Street Research has issued a "buy" rating with a price target of $105. Trading on Nasdaq is due to begin on Thursday. Nvidia's [better-than-expected quarterly results](/markets/nvidia-q3-earnings-record-revenue-after-huang/) on Tuesday lifted AI stocks ahead of the listing. New US data also showed [productivity grew 3.6% in the third quarter](/economy/us-productivity-q3-2026-ai-boom-workers/) while workers' share of output fell, which fits the "jobless growth" pattern described below.

## Key points

- The offering could raise about $64.35 billion at the top of its price range, Fortune reported. That is nearly twice the total raised by every US IPO in the previous quarter.
- Capital Economics said on Monday that the AI stock boom is in its final stages and called the listing "a key test".
- US employers added an average of 48,000 jobs a month in the year to September, far fewer than growth of this pace would normally produce.
- A Federal Reserve governor has warned of a "large correction" in share prices if returns on AI fall short.

## The listing

Bloomberg reported on 12 November that the offering was about six times oversubscribed, meaning investors asked for six times as many shares as are on sale. Nvidia is buying about $10 billion of Anthropic's stock alongside the offering, according to Fortune.

Reports of the company's overall valuation differ, and we have not been able to reconcile them, so we are not giving a figure here.

Joe Maher, markets economist at Capital Economics, pointed to falling share issuance and "investors' waning appetite for new shares of AI-firms at lofty valuations". A large listing tests that appetite directly: if the shares trade well, the boom has further to run. If they do not, other AI stocks are likely to fall with them.

## The warnings

**From the Federal Reserve.** Governor Michelle Bowman said in a speech on 10 November that share prices could suffer a "large correction" if AI does not deliver the returns investors expect, Semafor reported. She cited about $800 billion of AI-related debt, as well as liabilities kept off company balance sheets and the circular financing in which AI companies fund each other's purchases. A shock, she said, would slow the building of AI infrastructure and weaken the wider economy.

**From a former regulator.** Gary Gensler, who chaired the Securities and Exchange Commission until 2025, told CNN that the boom is a bubble and that he expects prices to fall and the economy to soften before the 2028 presidential election.

**On the other side.** Calling something a bubble does not mean the technology is worthless. The MIT economist Ricardo Caballero has said he has no doubt AI will be transformative, and that this "does not preclude a financial bubble". Railways and the internet both changed the economy and both ruined many early investors.

The International Monetary Fund's managing director raised the same concern about circular financing last week, as we reported in [the IMF's warning on AI financing](/economy/imf-georgieva-ai-circular-financing-warning/).

## Growth without jobs

The more unusual part of the picture is in the labour market.

| Measure | Latest figure |
|---|---|
| US GDP growth, third quarter, annual rate | 3.4% |
| Average monthly job gains, year to September | 48,000 |
| Jobs added in October | 165,000 |
| Unemployment rate, all workers | 4.1% |
| Unemployment rate, ages 20 to 24 | 7.5% |
| Productivity growth, third quarter, annual rate | 3.6% |
| Labour's share of output | 53.0%, the lowest since records began in 1947 |

Economists at Goldman Sachs call this "jobless growth". Spending on AI accounted for more than half of the growth in business investment in the first three quarters of 2026, by the bank's estimate.

There are two explanations, and both are probably true. David Kelly of J.P. Morgan Asset Management blames a sharp reversal in immigration policy, which has cut the supply of workers. Others point to AI itself.

## What AI has done to work so far

The evidence is early and mixed.

- A study by the Stanford Digital Economy Lab in August found employment among 22- to 25-year-olds in the jobs most exposed to AI was about 19% lower than it would otherwise have been.
- Researchers at Apollo found that since 2023, real wages in the most-exposed occupations have grown about 6.7 percentage points less than in other jobs, with no measurable fall in employment. They concluded that the first effect of AI has been "wage compression rather than employment displacement", and cautioned that the pandemic's after-effects may distort the comparison.
- Data from the jobs site Indeed points the other way for new hires: advertised salaries in the most-exposed occupations are up 46% since 2021, against 25% in the least exposed.

In short, productivity is rising and pay is not keeping up. Real wages have fallen year on year for seven consecutive months, according to J.P. Morgan Asset Management.

## What forecasters expect

Morgan Stanley expects US growth of 2.5% in 2027 and Goldman Sachs 2.4%, both supported by AI investment. J.P. Morgan Asset Management is more cautious, forecasting 1.6% over the next four quarters with hiring stuck near 50,000 a month.

None of these is a recession forecast. The risk the warnings describe is different: that a fall in AI share prices cuts the investment that is currently holding growth up.

## Why it matters in India

**Markets.** Foreign investors move money in and out of Indian shares according to conditions in the US. A sharp fall in American technology stocks usually brings selling in India too, whatever the state of Indian companies.

**IT services.** India's largest software exporters earn most of their revenue from US clients. Weak hiring and cautious budgets there affect orders here.

**Savers.** Anyone holding US technology funds, or Indian funds that invest abroad, is exposed to this trade. Spreading money across markets and asset types is the standard protection, as our guide to [index funds and active funds](/markets/index-funds-vs-active-funds/) explains.
