New York Federal Reserve President John Williams said on Monday that the central bank still has "work to do" to bring inflation under control, but suggested further action can unfold gradually as the economy keeps growing.

"Getting inflation back to 2% is 100% essential," Williams said in a speech at the University of Mount Saint Vincent in New York. "We must take actions over time that will ensure that we get inflation back to our 2% goal in a timely manner and on a sustained basis."

## Key points

- Williams expects inflation to move closer to the Fed's 2% target in 2027 and reach it in 2028.
- He blamed current inflation on the Iran war, demand linked to the AI build-out and tariffs, though he said tariff effects "have largely abated".
- He said there is "little evidence" that AI will cause more persistent inflation.
- Markets fully price in a 0.25 percentage point rate rise at the Fed's meeting on 8–9 December, from the current range of 3.75%–4%.
- His remarks were expected to be the last major policy statement from a Fed official before that meeting.

## Why Williams matters

As president of the New York Fed, Williams is a permanent voting member of the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), which sets US interest rates, and its vice chair. He is seen as close to the Fed's leadership, so markets read his comments as a guide to the committee's thinking.

Fed officials have disagreed over how fast to raise rates. Some want aggressive action. Williams has favoured a measured approach. He did not directly address the December decision but said data since the October meeting "show that the economy has remained strong and that inflation has continued to run too high".

## The shocks the Fed faces

"Monetary policy cannot move ships or reopen pipelines and refineries," Williams said, "but it can — and must — ensure that these shocks do not lead to broader or persistent inflationary dynamics."

| Source of inflation | Williams's view |
|---|---|
| Iran war and energy prices | A major current driver |
| AI build-out | Raising demand for some goods; little sign of lasting inflation |
| Tariffs | Effects have "largely abated", though new tariffs are being prepared |

New tariffs could add to price pressure. The US is preparing [higher Section 301 tariffs on China, the EU and others](/economy/us-tariff-hike-china-eu-section-301/).

## What a rate hike means

- **Borrowers:** mortgages, car loans and credit card rates tend to rise.
- **Property:** higher rates have already frozen many deals, with [US commercial real estate activity down 14% in October](/property/commercial-real-estate-activity-falls-october-interest-rates/).
- **Markets:** growth stocks and bonds are sensitive to rate rises. Ten-year Treasury yields are already around 5%, as we reported in our [story on yields and the Fed](/markets/treasury-yields-fed-rate-hike-december-ai-inflation/).

## Why it matters for India

Higher US interest rates tend to strengthen the dollar and can pull foreign investment out of emerging markets, including Indian shares and bonds. That can weaken the rupee and make imports, especially oil, more expensive. The Reserve Bank of India watches the Fed closely when setting its own policy.
