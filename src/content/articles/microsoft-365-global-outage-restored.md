Microsoft said on Saturday, 14 November, that its services had been fully restored after an outage that left office workers around the world without email, chat or meetings for hours.

The disruption began around midday on Thursday, US time, and affected Microsoft 365, the suite that includes Outlook, Teams and the Copilot AI assistant. It was one of the longest outages in the company's history and its second significant disruption this year, according to the financial news site Seeking Alpha.

## Key points

- Outlook email, Teams and Copilot were disrupted for hours, with full recovery confirmed two days later.
- The outage tracker Downdetector logged reports from more than 70 countries and territories.
- Microsoft says the cause was a problem introduced during a network upgrade. It says there was no cyberattack.
- Governments, hospitals, courts, police forces and banks were among those affected.

## What Microsoft says happened

In its statement, Microsoft said the incident "was introduced by an issue during an earlier network capacity upgrade that surfaced" after a more recent network change. During automated checks, servers were "unexpectedly unavailable".

In plain terms: the company had expanded part of its network some time earlier, and that work contained a fault that stayed hidden until a later change exposed it.

Reports citing Bloomberg said the problem mainly hit Exchange Online, the service behind Outlook email and calendars, and that the systems handling mail ran short of resources as they were brought back. That would explain why recovery was gradual. Microsoft apologised on Friday and said restoring services had taken longer than expected.

The company said the outage was "not related to cyber" and was not caused by AI agents, and that there was "no indication" of malicious activity. It has promised a full account in a post-incident report, so the details so far should be treated as preliminary.

## Who was affected

More than 430 million people use Microsoft 365 apps, according to the company. The effects were visible well beyond office email:

- **Governments.** Japan, Singapore, Australia, Greece and Norway reported disruption to email at some ministries or agencies. The British government said it was working with Microsoft.
- **Healthcare.** Britain's National Health Service was affected, and pharmacies reported delays in handling prescriptions.
- **Courts and police.** A trial in Manchester, England, was halted. British police could not receive crime reports online.
- **Banks.** Some banks in Hong Kong reported disruption.
- **Other technology firms.** OpenAI and Anthropic reported problems connecting their products to Microsoft 365 tools.

Jeremy Singer, a professor of computing at the University of Glasgow, said many office workers had been given a "virtual snow day". He said the episode showed how dependent organisations had become on one company's infrastructure.

## Was it connected to the US flight shutdown?

The day after the outage began, the US aviation regulator halted all departures nationwide. The coincidence led many people to suspect a link, or an attack.

Microsoft said it had no indication that the ground stop was related to any of its services. US officials have attributed that failure to their own ageing communications equipment. We cover it in [our report on the ground stop](/world/us-flights-cancelled-nationwide-ground-stop-recovery/).

The two events do share a pattern, which we look at in [our analysis of a week of failures](/tech/is-everything-broken-outages-week/): both began during an upgrade to a network.

## Lessons for businesses

For any company that runs on a single cloud suite, and that includes most Indian firms from large IT services companies to small offices, the outage is a useful test of preparation.

1. **Have a second way to reach people.** Keep an up-to-date list of staff and key customer phone numbers outside the email system. Agree in advance which backup channel the team will use.
2. **Know what else depends on your login.** Many other applications sign users in through their Microsoft account. When that fails, they fail too. List them.
3. **Keep offline copies of what matters this week.** Critical documents, contact lists and the day's schedule should be reachable without a connection.
4. **Read your service agreement.** Cloud contracts usually compensate for downtime with credits against a future bill, not cash, and often only if the customer applies. The credit rarely matches the cost of a lost working day.
5. **Subscribe to the provider's status alerts.** Knowing within minutes that the fault is at the provider's end saves hours of checking your own systems.
6. **Practise.** A short drill once a year, working for an hour without the main system, shows the gaps more clearly than any written plan.

None of this means leaving the cloud. Large providers are still more reliable than most companies could manage on their own servers. The point is that reliability is not the same as certainty, and a plan for the bad day costs very little.

## What to watch next

- **Microsoft's post-incident report**, which should set out the root cause and what will change.
- **Customer claims** for service credits.
- **Regulators' questions.** Several governments were directly affected, and dependence on a few cloud providers is already a concern for financial regulators in many countries.
