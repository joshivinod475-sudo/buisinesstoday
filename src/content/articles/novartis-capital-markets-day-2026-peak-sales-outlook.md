Novartis raised its forecast for peak sales of three of its biggest growth drugs to a combined $18 billion, up from $14 billion, at an investor event on Thursday. But its shares fell 2.4% in Swiss trading, as investors focused on a slightly more cautious longer-term growth outlook.

## Key points

- Peak sales forecast for Kesimpta, Scemblix and Leqvio raised to $18 billion from $14 billion.
- Novartis now expects sales to grow 5% a year through 2031, compared with previous guidance of 5% to 6% through 2030.
- The outlook reflects expected US generic competition for the breast cancer drug Kisqali from the second half of 2031.
- Citi said the 2031 guidance was about 3% above analysts' expectations.
- The company has suffered two major trial failures this year.

## The three drugs

| Drug | Treats | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Kesimpta | Multiple sclerosis | Gaining market share |
| Scemblix | A type of leukaemia | Gaining market share |
| Leqvio | High cholesterol | Strong demand in the US and Asia |

Chief executive Vas Narasimhan said the upgrades came from how the drugs are selling. "It's really that in-market commercial performance that's driving these upgrades," he told reporters.

## Why shares fell

The main question for investors is not the next five years but what comes after. Large drugmakers face a "patent cliff" when their best-selling medicines lose protection and cheaper generic or biosimilar copies arrive. Kisqali, one of Novartis's biggest products, is expected to face US generic competition from 2031.

Citi analysts said they expect the market to stay sceptical about growth after 2031 until the company produces more results from its pipeline of drugs in development.

## A difficult year in the lab

Novartis has had several setbacks:

- In September, its cardiovascular drug pelacarsen failed to show improved heart outcomes in a major trial. We explained that result in our report on [the pelacarsen trial](/healthcare/novartis-pelacarsen-lpa-heart-drug-trial-fails/).
- The next day, del-desiran, a drug for a muscle-wasting disorder, failed in a late-stage trial. The shares fell nearly 11%, their worst day since 1987. Novartis had acquired the drug through a $12 billion purchase of Avidity Biosciences earlier in the year.

Narasimhan defended the company's acquisition strategy, saying investors had differing views on dealmaking. He pointed to a 275% total shareholder return over the past decade as evidence that the approach works.

## What it means for investors and India

**For investors**, the pattern is typical of big pharma: current sales are strong, but the share price depends on confidence in the pipeline. Upgrades to existing drugs do not offset doubts about what replaces them.

**For India**, patent expiries are an opportunity. Indian generic drugmakers are among the world's largest suppliers of copies of medicines that lose patent protection, especially in the US market. Drugs such as Kisqali reaching the end of their patent life later this decade are the kind of products Indian companies prepare for years in advance.
