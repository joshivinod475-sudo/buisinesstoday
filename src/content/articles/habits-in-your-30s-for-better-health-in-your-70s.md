Most people expect to feel weaker, stiffer and slower by their 70s, and to be living with at least one chronic illness. Researchers who study ageing increasingly argue that this is not inevitable, and that the decade that matters most for preventing it is the 30s.

"Most people could expect to live to 90 or even 95 in good health if they were to optimise their lifestyle," Eric Verdin, chief executive of the Buck Institute for Research on Ageing in California, told BBC Future.

## Key points

- Muscle mass, bone density and metabolism start to show the first age-related changes in the 30s.
- The higher the peak you build then, the longer it lasts as you decline.
- Strength, aerobic fitness, flexibility, sleep and diet are the main levers.
- It is never too late to start, but starting earlier gives the most benefit.

## Why the 30s matter: building a reserve

Researchers often study masters athletes, people who keep competing in sport from their mid-30s into their 60s and beyond. Paul Morgan of Manchester Metropolitan University told the BBC that these athletes show a higher peak in heart and muscle function and a later decline. "They have this additional reserve which acts as a protective barrier," he said.

The idea is like a savings account. Everyone draws it down with age. The bigger the balance you build in your 30s, the longer it lasts before you fall below the level needed to live independently.

## The habits that matter most

**1. Strength training, especially the legs.** Falls are one of the biggest risks after 70, and they are linked to weak leg muscles and stiff joints. Two or three sessions a week of squats, lunges, step-ups and weight training build muscle and bone. You do not need a gym: body-weight exercises at home work.

**2. Aerobic fitness.** The maximum amount of oxygen your body can use during exercise is one of the strongest predictors of long life. Brisk walking, cycling, running, swimming or sport all count. Even a brisk 15-minute walk a day helps. Aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, as health authorities recommend.

**3. Flexibility and balance.** Yoga, stretching and balance exercises protect the range of movement in joints that makes everyday tasks possible later. Simple ankle and balance work is described in our guide to [strengthening your ankles](/healthcare/how-to-strengthen-ankles-prevent-injury/).

**4. Regular sleep.** Consistent bed and wake times matter as well as the number of hours. Poor sleep is linked to weight gain, diabetes, heart disease and cognitive decline.

**5. Eat for the long term.** Enough protein to maintain muscle, plenty of vegetables, pulses and whole grains, and less sugar, refined food and alcohol. Indian diets can be low in protein, so pulses, dairy, eggs, soya and nuts are worth building into every meal.

**6. Know your numbers.** Your 30s are the time to check blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol and weight regularly. South Asians develop diabetes and heart disease earlier and at lower body weights than many other groups, so early screening is especially useful in India.

**7. Do not smoke, and keep alcohol low.**

## The financial parallel

The same logic applies to money. Habits started in your 30s, such as regular investing, have decades to compound, just as fitness does. Health in later life is also one of the largest financial risks: long illness is expensive, and good health extends working years and independence. ## If you are past your 30s

Researchers stress that improvement is possible at any age. Strength training helps people in their 70s and 80s, and starting to walk regularly in middle age still lowers the risk of heart disease.
