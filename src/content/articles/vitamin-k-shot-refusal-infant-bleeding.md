Almost every baby born in a hospital is offered a single injection of vitamin K within hours of birth. It has been standard practice in the United States since 1961, and it prevents a rare but dangerous bleeding disorder.

Two large studies published on 5 November report that more American parents are now declining it, and that more babies are bleeding as a result.

## Key points

- The share of US newborns who did not receive vitamin K rose from 3.1% in 2017 to 6.8% in 2025, a study in the journal JAMA found.
- Diagnoses of vitamin K deficiency bleeding rose 347% over a similar period, a second study in JAMA Pediatrics found.
- Babies who did not get the injection had more than twice the risk of bleeding in the brain.
- The injection is a vitamin supplement. It is not a vaccine.

## Why newborns need vitamin K

Vitamin K is what the body uses to make blood clot. Adults get it from food and from bacteria in the gut. Newborns have very little: only small amounts cross the placenta before birth, breast milk contains little, and a baby's gut has not yet developed the bacteria that produce it.

Without enough of it, a baby can bleed without any injury. This is called vitamin K deficiency bleeding, or VKDB. It comes in three forms:

- **Early**, within 24 hours of birth, usually linked to medicines the mother took in pregnancy.
- **Classical**, between two days and one week, often showing as bruising or bleeding from the umbilical cord.
- **Late**, from one week to six months and most often between two and eight weeks. This is the most dangerous form because the bleeding frequently occurs in the brain.

Some babies show warning signs such as bruising, nosebleeds, pale skin or poor feeding. Others show none before a serious bleed. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates that one in five babies who develop the condition dies.

A single injection into the thigh, usually within six hours of birth, prevents it.

## What the two studies found

**The JAMA Pediatrics study**, by researchers at Virginia Commonwealth University, examined the medical records of more than 11 million infants from 2017 to 2025. The share of newborns receiving the injection fell from 97.7% to 95%. Over the same period, diagnoses of vitamin K deficiency bleeding rose by 347.1%.

**The JAMA study**, led by Kristan Scott of the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, followed more than 5.2 million infants. It found:

- the share not receiving the injection more than doubled, from 3.1% in 2017 to 6.8% in 2025
- babies who did not receive it had a 47% higher risk of bleeding in their first six months, after adjusting for other factors
- their risk of bleeding in the brain was more than twice as high
- there was one additional bleeding event for every 114 newborns who went without

A caution on what this shows. Both studies found an association in medical records. They cannot prove that refusal caused each individual case. But the direction is consistent with decades of evidence on what vitamin K does.

The studies also point to a gap in the data. The CDC does not routinely track either refusals or cases, so there is no official national count. In July two members of the US Congress asked the agency to begin collecting and publishing the figures, after an investigation by the news organisation ProPublica found babies who had died after their parents declined the injection.

## Why parents say no

According to the research and to doctors quoted in US coverage, the reasons include:

- the mistaken belief that the injection is a vaccine
- worry about its ingredients, including preservatives
- not wanting a newborn to feel pain
- general distrust of medical procedures and a preference for "natural" options
- claims circulating on social media

Medical bodies say the injection has a strong safety record built over more than 60 years. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends it for every newborn.

Oral vitamin K drops are sometimes suggested as an alternative. In the US they are not considered equivalent, because they must be given in several doses and do not reliably protect against the late form of bleeding.

## If a baby did not get the injection

Dr David Hill, a paediatrician with the American Academy of Pediatrics, told Newsweek that the risk lasts for up to six months, so a later dose is still better than none. It matters most before any surgery, including circumcision.

His practical advice for parents:

- check the hospital or birth records if you are unsure
- contact your doctor promptly, since hospitals stock the injection and many clinics do not
- treat unexplained bruising, bleeding or unusual paleness in a young baby as urgent

## What Indian parents should know

In India, a vitamin K injection at birth is part of standard newborn care under national guidelines and is given routinely in government and private hospitals.

Three points are worth knowing:

- **Ask at delivery.** Confirm with the doctor or nurse that the injection has been given, and that it is recorded on the baby's discharge summary or card.
- **Home births are the gap.** A baby born outside a hospital may miss it. A doctor can give it afterwards.
- **It is not part of the vaccine schedule.** It is a one-time supplement given at birth and is separate from the immunisations that follow.

The wider pattern, of parents declining a long-established protection because of what they have read online, is the same one behind falling vaccination rates and the [return of measles in the United States](/healthcare/us-loses-measles-elimination-status/).
