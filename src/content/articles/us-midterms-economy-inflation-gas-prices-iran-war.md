By many measures, the US economy is in good shape. Unemployment is low enough for economists to call it full employment. Consumer spending has held up. AI investment is driving what is projected to be the largest spending boom in modern US history.

Yet in the 3 November midterm elections, voters turned sharply against President Donald Trump's Republicans, two years after electing them to fix the economy. The explanation, according to an analysis by CNN, comes down to prices, and above all to the cost of fuel after the US went to war with Iran.

## Key points

- Inflation returned in 2026 after falling close to a four-year low early in the year.
- After the war with Iran began, oil hit four-year highs, diesel reached a record and petrol was the most expensive ever on an Election Day.
- Higher petrol and diesel prices have cost the average US household about $1,150 since the war began, according to Brown University researchers.
- Wages have grown more slowly than prices since March, so real pay is falling.
- Mortgage rates have risen at their fastest pace in three years.

## Early 2026: a good story

At the start of the year, Republicans had a strong economic case:

- January brought the best month of job gains since 2024.
- Manufacturing jobs rose for the first time in 14 months.
- Large tax refunds from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act reached households.
- Mortgage rates fell below 6% for the first time in three years.
- Stocks hit records, and inflation was close to its lowest in four years.

## Then the war

"If it weren't for the war in Iran, this would be a very different midterm election," said Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union.

Oil prices surged, and fuel became the most visible symbol of rising costs. Americans notice petrol prices more than most others: they are displayed on large signs and paid every week. As energy costs rose, bond yields and mortgage rates followed.

After three years in which pay rises outpaced price rises, the trend reversed in March. The average American's pay, adjusted for inflation, has fallen since Trump returned to office. In his first term, by contrast, wage growth was nearly double inflation.

Voters also linked other policies to their costs: tariffs, light-touch regulation of AI and data centres, and immigration restrictions that business groups say have tightened labour supply.

## Why the data and the mood diverge

Headline numbers average out very different experiences. Higher-income households, whose wealth has grown with share prices, are spending freely. Middle- and lower-income households spend more on necessities and feel price rises most. October's [retail sales figures](/economy/us-retail-sales-october-2026-consumers/) showed that split, with grocery volumes falling as food prices rose.

## What it means from January

- **Congress changes hands.** Democrats will control both chambers, with investigations promised into issues from the [Nvidia–Huang affair](/tech/jensen-huang-senate-banking-testimony-lutnick-megaspeed/) to tariffs.
- **States act on data centres.** New Democratic governors in Texas and elsewhere have proposed [tighter rules on data centre construction](/world/texas-election-data-centre-boom-democrats-ai-moratorium/).
- **Inflation pressure remains.** The Federal Reserve is now expected to [raise interest rates in December](/markets/treasury-yields-fed-rate-hike-december-ai-inflation/).

## Lessons beyond America

Fuel prices are a political force in many countries, India included, where petrol and diesel prices are closely watched and heavily taxed. The US result shows how quickly an energy shock can override other good economic news when it reaches household budgets every week.
