United Airlines held departing flights at airports across the United States for more than 90 minutes on Monday, 16 November, after a failure in its own computer systems.

The airline said the problem was fixed about an hour and 40 minutes after pilots first reported it to air traffic controllers, the Associated Press reported. United said there was "no indication that this was a cybersecurity-related issue".

It came three days after a fault in the US air traffic control network [halted flights nationwide](/world/us-flights-cancelled-nationwide-ground-stop-recovery/). The two events are not known to be connected.

## Key points

- United described a "widespread system outage" affecting departing flights.
- Flights already in the air were not affected, and neither were United Express regional services, the airline said.
- The tracking service FlightAware showed about 600 United flights delayed on Monday evening. It is not clear how many were caused by the outage.
- United has not said which system failed.

## What went wrong

United has not named the system. Pilots told air traffic controllers that the problem involved software that helps work out an aircraft's final weight, according to the AP.

In a recording from one airport, a United pilot in Houston asked the tower for permission to stay at the gate until the system was working again.

## Why a plane cannot leave without those numbers

Before every departure, pilots use performance software to confirm that the aircraft can take off safely from that runway in that weather. The calculation depends on the weight of the aircraft and everything on it: fuel, passengers, baggage and cargo, and on how that weight is distributed.

The result sets the speeds at which the pilots will lift off and how much runway they need. If the figures are missing or uncertain, the correct decision is not to go.

Steve Arroyo, an aviation safety expert and former United pilot, told the AP: "It is extremely important to have all of these numbers accurate and precise." He said the airline was right to delay flights.

## It has happened before

In August 2025, United grounded aircraft at major US airports and more than 1,000 flights were delayed. That outage involved a system called Unimatic, which feeds flight information to other systems, including those that calculate weight and balance.

United told the AP it had no further information on whether the same system was involved this time.

## A bad week for critical systems

Monday's outage was one of several unrelated failures in a few days.

- On Thursday, a [Microsoft outage](/tech/microsoft-365-global-outage-restored/) disrupted Outlook email.
- On Friday, the Federal Aviation Administration ordered a nationwide ground stop after a network fault. We explained [why a ground stop disrupts flights far from its cause](/world/why-ground-stop-disrupts-flights-far-away/).
- On Monday, a software bug at Chase caused [problems for customers of several US banks](/markets/us-bank-outage-chase-software-bug-zelle/).

Airlines depend on a chain of older and newer systems that must all work together: reservations, crew scheduling, flight planning and weight calculation. A fault in one link stops the aircraft even when everything else is working.

## What passengers can do

**Check the airline's app before leaving for the airport.** It is usually updated before the departure boards.

**Know that delays outlast the outage.** Aircraft and crews end up in the wrong places, so flights later in the day are affected.

**Keep receipts.** If you are stranded overnight, ask the airline what it will cover, and keep proof of what you spent.

**Travellers from India** connecting through United's hubs, such as Newark, Chicago and San Francisco, should allow longer connections during periods of disruption and check whether their ticket is on a single booking. On a single booking the airline must rebook a missed connection. On separate tickets it need not.
