On Friday, 13 November, a problem that officials traced to equipment in El Dorado, Arkansas, and Amarillo, Texas, stopped aircraft from taking off in New York, Los Angeles and everywhere in between.

To a passenger stuck at a gate 2,000 km from either town, that makes little sense. The explanation lies in how air traffic control is organised: it is one connected network, and a network can fail all at once.

## The short answer

- Aircraft at cruising height are guided by a small number of regional centres that must talk to each other constantly.
- Those centres are linked by one shared communications network. A fault in that network can reach most of them.
- When controllers cannot be sure of talking to pilots, the only safe response is to stop new flights taking off.
- Once aircraft and crews are in the wrong places, the timetable takes days to rebuild.

## How the system is organised

Air traffic control in the US works in layers.

**Towers** handle take-offs and landings at an airport. **Approach control** manages aircraft climbing away from or descending towards a busy area. Above about 10,000 feet, flights are handed to one of the country's **regional control centres**, each responsible for a large block of sky, experts told the Associated Press.

There are 22 of these regional centres. An aircraft crossing the country passes from one to the next, and at every handover the two centres must exchange information about that flight.

All of this depends on a communications network that AP described as "one of the largest telecommunications systems in the country". It carries controllers' radio links to aircraft, radar data and the messages between centres.

## Why the failure spread

A storm or a power cut usually affects one airport or one region. Friday's fault was different because it sat in the shared network.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said a contractor was replacing old copper lines with fibre-optic cable. After a router was switched on in Arkansas, a problem appeared at a facility in Texas and cascaded. The FAA said the result was "intermittent" communications and gaps in radar coverage. According to AP, unstable radio frequencies affected up to 17 of the 22 regional centres.

John Hansman, a professor of aeronautics at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, told AP the failure was not a local one and "propagated around the country".

This is the trade-off in any tightly connected system. Linking everything together makes it efficient. It also means a fault in the links is a fault everywhere.

## Why stopping everything was the safe choice

A ground stop holds aircraft at their departure airports. It does not affect those already flying.

The logic is simple. The safest place for an aircraft during a communications problem is on the ground. By stopping departures, controllers reduce the number of aircraft they must manage with unreliable equipment and can concentrate on landing those in the air. More than 3,000 flights were airborne when the order was given on Friday, and all landed safely.

Aviation is built with several layers of backup for exactly this situation.

- Pilots can talk to each other and to nearby controllers by radio on other frequencies.
- Aircraft carry systems that detect other aircraft and warn of a collision risk.
- A control tower that loses its radios can signal pilots with high-intensity coloured light beams.

"We have a number of backup systems that allow the airplanes to fly safely," Hansman told AP.

## Why the delays last for days

The stop lasted a little over four hours. Cancellations continued through the weekend. Three things explain the gap.

**Aircraft are in the wrong places.** An airliner typically flies four to six legs a day. If the first is cancelled, the aircraft is not where its later flights begin.

**Crews run out of time.** Pilots and cabin crew have legal limits on working hours. A crew that waits four hours on the ground may no longer be allowed to complete its day, and replacements may be in another city.

**There are few spare seats.** Flights run nearly full, so passengers from cancelled flights cannot all be moved to the next one.

Each of these knocks on to the next day. That is why airlines cancel in advance during a recovery: removing some flights lets them put aircraft and crews back in position for the rest.

## Could it happen elsewhere?

Every country's air traffic system has shared components, so the risk exists everywhere. What differs is the age of the equipment and how many backups there are.

India has had a smaller version of the same problem. In November 2025 a fault in the automatic messaging system used by air traffic control in Delhi delayed hundreds of flights while controllers processed flight plans by hand.

The pattern is common to much modern infrastructure, including payment systems. Our explainer on [what happens when you pay with UPI](/tech/what-happens-when-you-pay-with-upi/) describes another network where many separate organisations depend on shared links in the middle.

## What it means for travellers

- A problem far from your route can still cancel your flight. Check the status before leaving home.
- Early morning flights are the least exposed, since the aircraft is usually already at the airport from the night before.
- Direct flights carry less risk than connections during a disruption.
- If your flight is cancelled, rebook through the airline's app straight away, before spare seats go.
