Brick Lane in east London is one of Britain's best-known curry streets. For a first-time visitor, the menus can be confusing. The same dish name can be mild in one restaurant and fiery in the next, and "medium" means different things to different cooks.

A few minutes spent reading the menu and talking to staff usually prevents an uncomfortable meal. Here is how to do it.

## Key points

- The British curry house menu has a rough heat ladder: **korma** at the mild end, then dishes such as **tikka masala**, **bhuna** and **dopiaza**, then **madras**, **jalfrezi** and **vindaloo**, with **phall** at the extreme.
- The ladder is a guide only. Recipes, chilli types and portion sizes differ between kitchens.
- Many Brick Lane menus mark dishes as mild, medium or hot. Ask staff when the label is unclear.
- Most curry houses can make a dish milder or hotter on request.
- By law in the UK, restaurants must tell you if a dish contains any of 14 major allergens, including nuts, milk and mustard.

## Why Brick Lane menus look the way they do

Most of the restaurants on Brick Lane were founded by people from **Sylhet** in north-east Bangladesh. Bengali seafarers settled in the East End in the early 20th century and opened cafés and lodging houses, and the curry trade grew from there. The area around the street has been promoted as **Banglatown** since the late 1990s.

The menus these restaurants developed for British diners became the standard "curry house" menu found across the UK. Many dishes, such as balti and chicken tikka masala, were shaped in Britain rather than in South Asia. The number of curry houses on Brick Lane has fallen sharply since the mid-2000s, as rents rose and the pandemic hit, according to the [Beyond Banglatown research project](https://www.beyondbanglatown.org.uk/globe/cookbooks-cafes-curry-restaurants/).

## The rough heat ladder

| Heat | Typical dishes | What gives the flavour |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | Korma, pasanda, butter chicken, tikka masala | Cream, yoghurt, ground nuts, coconut |
| Mild, dry | Tandoori chicken, chicken or paneer tikka | Yoghurt and spice marinades, cooked in a clay oven |
| Medium | Bhuna, dopiaza, rogan josh, many vegetable dishes | Tomato, onion and whole spices |
| Hot | Madras, jalfrezi with fresh green chillies | Chilli powder and fresh chillies |
| Very hot | Vindaloo, dishes made with naga chillies | Large amounts of chilli, sometimes naga pickle |
| Extreme | Phall | A British curry house creation designed to be as hot as possible |

The names do not always match their origins. **Vindaloo** comes from a Goan dish influenced by Portuguese cooking, with vinegar and garlic. In Goa it is tangy and spiced, not necessarily the hottest thing on the menu. In British curry houses it became shorthand for "very hot".

**Naga chillies** deserve special care. The naga or bhut jolokia, grown in north-east India and Bangladesh, was certified by Guinness World Records as the world's hottest chilli in 2007, measuring over one million Scoville heat units. It has since been overtaken, but it is still far hotter than ordinary green chillies. Sylheti restaurants often use it, especially in dishes described as "naga" or "Sylhet style".

## How to check before you order

**Read the description, not just the name.** Look for words such as "fresh green chillies", "naga", "fiery" or "Kashmiri chilli". Cream, coconut, yoghurt, almonds and cashews usually point to a gentler sauce.

**Look for symbols.** Many menus use chilli icons or labels. Note how many levels the menu uses, because one chilli on one menu can be hotter than two on another.

**Ask the staff specific questions.** Rather than asking "is it spicy?", try:

- "On a scale of one to five, how hot is this?"
- "Does it have fresh chillies or naga in it?"
- "Can the kitchen make it milder?"

**Order to share.** A table can mix a mild korma with a hotter jalfrezi and order raita, a yoghurt dip, to cool things down. Rice, naan and yoghurt help more than water, because capsaicin, the compound that makes chillies hot, does not dissolve in water.

**Watch the extras.** Pickles, chutneys and green chilli sauces on the table can be much hotter than the curries.

## Allergies and dietary needs

Spice is a matter of taste. Allergies are a matter of safety. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, food businesses must give customers information if any of the **14 regulated allergens** are used in a dish, including peanuts, tree nuts, milk, sesame, mustard and celery. The Food Standards Agency's [guidance on non-prepacked food](https://www.food.gov.uk/business-guidance/allergen-information-for-non-prepacked-foods-best-practice-introduction) recommends written information backed up by a conversation with staff.

Nuts are common in Indian restaurant cooking, especially in korma, pasanda and some marinades. Tell staff about any allergy before ordering, not just a preference for mild food.

## For Indian visitors

Indian travellers in London are often surprised by curry house food. Dishes such as chicken tikka masala, balti and phall are British creations, and the flavours tend to be sweeter and creamier than home cooking in many parts of India. If you want something closer to regional Indian or Bangladeshi food, ask whether the kitchen has a home-style dish or a Bangladeshi speciality not listed under the standard curries.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the mildest curry on a British Indian menu?**
Korma is usually the mildest, followed by pasanda and tikka masala.

**Is vindaloo the hottest curry?**
On most British menus it is near the top. Phall and dishes made with naga chillies are often hotter.

**Can I ask a Brick Lane restaurant to make a curry milder?**
Yes. Most curry houses cook to order and can adjust the heat.

**What helps if a curry is too hot?**
Yoghurt, raita, rice and bread work better than water.
