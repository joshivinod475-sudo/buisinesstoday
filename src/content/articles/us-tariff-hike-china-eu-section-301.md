The United States is preparing to announce higher tariffs on goods from China, the European Union and more than a dozen other economies, possibly as soon as Tuesday, Bloomberg reported, citing a person familiar with the matter. The move is meant to replace tariffs that the Supreme Court struck down earlier this year.

The new duties are expected to target economies accused of hurting American industry by subsidising their own manufacturers and producing more than markets need.

## Key points

- The Office of the US Trade Representative (USTR) is expected to conclude an investigation into "structural excess capacity" under **Section 301** of the Trade Act of 1974.
- The investigation, opened in March 2026, covers 16 economies, including China and the EU.
- Section 301 lets the president impose tariffs to respond to unfair trade practices without new legislation.
- The Supreme Court struck down Trump's earlier tariffs, imposed under emergency powers (IEEPA), on 20 February 2026.
- Exact rates and products had not been announced at the time of the report.

## Why the tariffs are being rebuilt

In 2025, President Trump imposed sweeping tariffs using the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA). The Supreme Court ruled in February that IEEPA did not give him that power. That ruling triggered a fight over refunds, which we covered in our report on [tariff refunds for companies and consumers](/economy/ftc-tariff-refunds-companies-consumers-senators-letter/).

The administration responded by using other laws:

| Law | What it allows |
|---|---|
| Section 122, Trade Act of 1974 | Temporary tariffs of up to 15% for up to 150 days to deal with balance-of-payments problems |
| Section 232, Trade Expansion Act of 1962 | Tariffs on specific products for national security reasons, such as steel and aluminium |
| Section 301, Trade Act of 1974 | Tariffs on countries found to use unfair trade practices, after an investigation |

Section 301 tariffs are slower to impose, because they require an investigation and public comment. But they are on firmer legal ground, and they were used for the first round of US tariffs on China in 2018.

## Who could be affected

The March investigation named 16 trading partners suspected of creating excess manufacturing capacity through subsidies, cheap credit or state support. China is the main target, but the inclusion of the EU means allies will be hit too. Expect retaliation threats from Beijing and Brussels.

## What it means for markets and inflation

- **Prices:** higher tariffs raise costs for US importers and consumers. Inflation is already above the Federal Reserve's target, and the Fed is expected to [raise interest rates in December](/economy/john-williams-fed-inflation-december-rate-hike/).
- **Trade flows:** companies may shift supply chains again, which can benefit countries not targeted.
- **Stocks:** exporters and retailers that depend on imports are most exposed.

## What it means for India

Whether India is among the 16 economies matters greatly. If India faces lower tariffs than China or the EU, Indian exporters of electronics, textiles, pharmaceuticals and engineering goods could gain market share in the US. If it is included, exporters will face higher costs in their largest export market. India and the US have been negotiating a trade agreement, and the outcome of these tariffs will shape those talks.
