The Port of New Orleans has gone to court to take control of a tanker that struck a docked river cruise boat on the Mississippi River, tipping it on to its side and throwing dozens of people into the water.

The port's Board of Commissioners filed the lawsuit on Friday, 13 November, within hours of the collision. A judge issued a warrant the same day placing the tanker in the hands of federal officials for now.

Everyone on board both vessels survived.

## Key points

- The tanker Atlantic Asphalt hit the cruise paddleboat American Heritage at the port's Thalia Street terminal on Friday afternoon.
- All 197 passengers and crew on the cruise boat survived. Thirty-two people were taken to hospital, none with critical injuries.
- The port says the crash caused at least $1 million of damage to its terminal and alleges negligence by the ship's owner and operators.
- Federal investigators expect a preliminary report within about a month.

## What happened

The American Heritage, a paddlewheel river cruise boat, was moored at the end of an eight-day trip along the Mississippi. The Atlantic Asphalt, a 160-metre tanker carrying asphalt and bitumen, was arriving in New Orleans.

Officials said the tanker lost control. It apparently lost steering, according to the Associated Press, and struck the cruise boat, which rolled on to its side against the terminal.

A passenger, Joe Knab, told AP that the ship's horn sounded several times before the impact and that about 100 people ended up in the river. He estimated he was in the water for about 15 minutes. Two police officers were among those thrown in.

The New Orleans harbour police chief, Melanie Montroll, said that getting passengers into life jackets before the boat rolled probably saved many lives.

Eight people were still in hospital on Saturday.

## Who owns the tanker

The Atlantic Asphalt sails under the flag of the Marshall Islands. Its registered owner is Nave Shipping SA, a company registered in Liberia, which AP reported is a subsidiary of China State Shipbuilding Corporation, a state-owned Chinese group. The lawsuit also names Thome Ship Management, a ship management company in Singapore.

The corporation did not respond to AP's request for comment.

That chain, an owner in one country, a flag from another and a manager in a third, is normal in commercial shipping. It is also the reason for what the port did next.

## Why the port wants the ship itself

In most lawsuits, you sue a person or a company. Maritime law allows something older and more direct: a claim against the ship.

When a vessel causes damage, those harmed can ask a court to "arrest" it, meaning it is held in port under a court order. The ship then serves as security for the claim. If the owner does not pay or post a bond, the court can order the vessel sold and the proceeds used to settle what is owed.

The rule exists because ships move. An owner may be a small company in a distant country with no other assets within reach. Once the vessel sails, there may be nothing left to claim against. Arresting it before it leaves is often the only practical way to make sure a judgment can be collected.

The port's lawsuit asks for exactly this, alleging that the collision resulted from negligence or carelessness by the ship and the companies operating it. Those are allegations, and the defendants have not yet answered them in court.

In practice, such cases usually end with the owner's insurers providing a guarantee so the ship can be released while the dispute continues.

## The investigation

The US National Transportation Safety Board has sent a 16-member team and has recovered the tanker's voyage data recorder, the shipping equivalent of an aircraft's black box. Board member John DeLeeuw said a preliminary report is expected in 24 to 30 days and a final report in 18 to 24 months.

The tanker has been towed away. The cruise boat is still on its side against the terminal, and the Coast Guard said moving it could take weeks. The first task is to remove about 60,000 litres of fuel from it safely.

## Why the river matters

The lower Mississippi is one of the busiest trade routes in the world. Much of the grain exported from the American Midwest travels down it to the ports around New Orleans, and oil, chemicals and fertiliser move along it in both directions.

Traffic was restricted after the collision. The Coast Guard said vessels should soon be able to move both upstream and downstream. A longer closure would have delayed cargoes and raised shipping costs, which is why the speed of the salvage work matters beyond the port itself.

## What to watch next

- **The court case:** whether the owner's insurers post security to free the tanker.
- **The preliminary report** from investigators, due in about a month, which should address the reported loss of steering.
- **Claims from passengers** and from the cruise operator, which are likely to follow the port's.
- **The salvage** of the American Heritage and the full reopening of the river.
