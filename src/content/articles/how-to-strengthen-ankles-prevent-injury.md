Ankles carry the body's weight with every step, absorb the shock of landing and keep us balanced. Most people never think about them until they twist one.

Ankle injuries account for about 5% of visits to emergency departments in the UK, according to the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence. Many could be avoided, says Genevieve Tawiah, a performance physiotherapist who spoke to the BBC, if people were stronger and more aware of how their feet and ankles move.

## Key points

- Start by improving your awareness of how your foot moves.
- Then build strength and balance with heel raises and balance walks.
- Practise control in everyday movements, such as standing up and climbing stairs.
- Wear shoes that give your toes room.

## Step 1: Awareness

Tawiah calls this being "brain to body aware": sensing what your ankle is doing without looking at it. Good movement starts in the toes, which affect how weight passes through the ankle.

Two exercises to try while watching your foot:

- **Big toe press.** Press the tip of your big toe into the floor and hold. You should feel the arch of your foot lift and engage.
- **Toe spread.** Place a pen on the floor alongside your big toe and try to move the toe towards it.

## Step 2: Strength and balance

- **Heel raises.** Stand next to a sturdy surface, rise onto your toes and lower slowly. Keep the ankles straight, without rolling outwards. Start with two sets of 10.
- **Balance walk.** Walk heel to toe in a straight line, as if on a beam, keeping control of each foot.
- **Single-leg stand.** Hold for 30 seconds on each side, near a wall for safety. Make it harder by closing your eyes or standing on a cushion.

## Step 3: Control in daily life

- **Sit to stand.** Rise from a chair without using your hands, first on two legs, later on one.
- **Stairs.** If you always lead with the same leg, practise leading with the other.

**After a sprain**, once a doctor or physiotherapist allows it, Tawiah suggests balancing on one leg while catching a ball, or stepping to the side and holding the landing.

**For chronic tendon pain**, known as tendinopathy, slow, heavy loading helps more than rest, for example calf raises holding weights. This is best done with a physiotherapist's guidance.

## Choosing shoes

Many people wear shoes that are narrow at the front, including pointed shoes and some trainers. Tawiah's test: imagine the top of the shoe was removed. Would your toes have space, or would your big toe be pushed inwards?

Barefoot-style shoes are popular and can help strengthen the feet, but switch to them gradually to avoid overloading tissues that are not used to the work.

## Why it matters more with age

Weak ankles and poor balance are a leading reason older people fall, and falls are a major cause of fractures and lost independence. The exercises above take a few minutes a day and need no equipment. They fit naturally into the routines we describe in [how to stay fit as you age](/healthcare/how-to-stay-fit-as-you-age/) and [habits in your 30s that protect your health later](/healthcare/habits-in-your-30s-for-better-health-in-your-70s/).

**See a doctor** if after an ankle injury you cannot put weight on the foot, the ankle looks misshapen, there is numbness, or swelling and pain do not improve within a few days.

*This article is general information and not medical advice. People with existing injuries, arthritis or balance problems should seek advice from a doctor or physiotherapist before starting new exercises. It draws on advice given by physiotherapist Genevieve Tawiah to the BBC.*
