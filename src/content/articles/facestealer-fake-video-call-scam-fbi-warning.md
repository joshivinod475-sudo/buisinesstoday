A video call has long been the way to check that someone is real. A scheme the FBI calls FaceStealer turns that habit against its victims.

The FBI says North Korean hackers stole more than $400 million in cryptocurrency over about 18 months by inviting people to fake video meetings, secretly recording their faces, and then using that footage to deceive the victims' own contacts. It has seized more than 900 such recordings, according to reports of its warning on 12 November.

## Key points

- Invitations come from hijacked accounts on Telegram, LinkedIn, Slack and Discord, so they appear to come from someone the target knows.
- The meeting runs on fake versions of Zoom, Microsoft Teams or Google Meet.
- During the call the victim is asked to install software or run a command to "fix" an audio problem. That installs malware.
- The victim's webcam is recorded, and the footage is used to lure the next victim.

## How the cycle works

1. **A trusted name.** You receive a meeting invitation from a colleague, investor or contact whose account has already been taken over.
2. **A familiar face.** You join and see that person on screen. It is a recording of them, captured on an earlier fake call, possibly altered with AI.
3. **A technical problem.** Your microphone "doesn't work", and you are sent a link or a command to fix it.
4. **The compromise.** The "fix" installs malware that steals passwords, crypto wallets and access to company systems.
5. **The next victim.** Your own face, recorded during the call, becomes bait for the people who trust you.

The FBI attributes the activity to North Korean state-backed groups, including those tracked as BlueNoroff, APT38 and Sapphire Sleet. North Korea denies such allegations. The main targets have been people in cryptocurrency and Web3 companies and maintainers of open-source software.

## The FBI's advice

According to reports of the warning, the FBI recommends:

- **Keep your camera off** until the person who invited you has appeared and you are satisfied they are real.
- **Verify the meeting on a separate channel.** Call or message the person using a number or account you already have, not the one that sent the invitation.
- **End the call** if you are asked to install software, click an update link or run a command. No genuine meeting needs this.
- **Join only through the official app** or the company's known web address, never through a downloaded "meeting client" you did not already have.

## Why this matters to banks and businesses

The same method threatens any organisation where a face on a screen is used to approve something: a payment authorised on a video call, a customer verified by video, a new employee onboarded remotely.

Banks have been moving towards video for identity checks, including India's video-based customer identification process for remote account opening. That process is a live session run by the bank's own staff with liveness checks, which is a different situation from a hijacked invitation. But the principle is the same: a video feed is evidence, not proof. ## For companies: practical controls

1. **No payments approved on video alone.** Require a second, independent confirmation for any transfer.
2. **Lock down software installation** on staff laptops, so a "fix" sent during a call cannot run.
3. **Approve meeting tools centrally** and block look-alike domains.
4. **Train staff with real examples.** The story of a familiar face asking for an urgent fix is more convincing than a policy document.
5. **Report quickly.** In India, cyber incidents must be reported to CERT-In within six hours, and financial fraud can be reported to the national cyber crime helpline, 1930.
