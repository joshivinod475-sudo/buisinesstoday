South Korea is rewriting its exam-room rules days before its most important test of the year, after the top scorer in a national professional exam admitted that he cheated with the help of artificial intelligence.

More than 500,000 students will sit the College Scholastic Ability Test, known as the Suneung, on Thursday, 19 November, at about 1,300 sites. The education minister has refused calls to postpone it.

## Key points

- A 34-year-old candidate who topped the national tax accountant exam has confessed to using a hidden camera, a smartphone and an AI tool, the Associated Press reported. Police are investigating.
- Each exam site will have at least one supervisor trained to look for smart devices.
- Officials are considering making candidates hand over devices and switching off public Wi-Fi at exam sites.
- The government's full set of measures was due to be announced on Tuesday.

## The case that caused the alarm

The candidate's scores drew attention after he discussed them in media interviews: 397.5 out of 400 on the multiple-choice stage in April, and 663 out of 800 on the essay stage in July. One Korean newspaper compared the result to running 100 metres in under five seconds.

In a video posted on his lawyer's YouTube channel on Sunday, he said he had photographed the questions with a concealed camera, sent them to a generative AI tool and read the answers on his phone. He said he would cooperate with the police.

Online speculation had focused on AI-equipped glasses. His account describes something harder to spot.

It was not the first incident. Candidates were caught using AI smart glasses in an English proficiency test in June and in some university essay exams in October, according to the AP.

## What will be different on Thursday

Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin said postponing the exam would cause "even a larger disruption" to students. The measures described so far are:

| Measure | Status |
|---|---|
| A trained supervisor at every site to watch for smart glasses | Confirmed |
| New guidance for proctors on hidden cameras in sleeves, collars and buttons | Issued |
| Metal detectors and radio-signal detectors | Limited use |
| Collecting candidates' smart devices | Under consideration |
| Switching off public Wi-Fi at exam sites | Under consideration |
| Restricting mobile signals at exam sites | Under discussion; South Korean law bans signal jamming |
| Thermal cameras | Unlikely, because of privacy concerns |

Supervisors cannot search a candidate's belongings without consent. Officials are also discussing whether to require manufacturers to make AI devices show a light or make a sound when in use.

## Why detection is so difficult

For months the authorities concentrated on smart glasses, which can usually be recognised by their thick frames. The tax exam case showed the weakness in that approach. A camera can be the size of a button, and a phone can be anywhere.

There are three ways to respond, and each has limits.

- **Look for the device.** Devices keep getting smaller, and searches raise questions of privacy and consent.
- **Cut the connection.** Blocking signals stops answers coming back, but it can be unlawful and affects everyone nearby.
- **Change the exam.** Oral tests, supervised practical work and questions that demand reasoning in person are harder to outsource to a machine. They are also slower and more expensive to run.

The Hankyoreh newspaper wrote in an editorial that AI could threaten "the very foundations of the examination system".

## Why the Suneung matters so much

The exam largely decides which university a student attends, and that in turn shapes careers. South Korea adjusts its day around it: offices and the stock market open later, flights are paused during the English listening section, and police escort students who are running late.

## The same question in India

India runs some of the largest competitive exams in the world, including JEE, NEET and the civil services exam, and has had its own cheating scandals involving hidden earpieces and leaked papers. Exam bodies already use frisking, signal jammers and biometric checks at many centres.

The South Korean case suggests the next problem is a candidate who needs no accomplice outside the hall, only a camera and a connection.
