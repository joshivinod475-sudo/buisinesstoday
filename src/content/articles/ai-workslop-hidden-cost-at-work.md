A colleague sends you a report. It is neatly formatted, confidently written and three pages long. Half an hour in, you realise it does not answer the question, one of its numbers is wrong, and you will have to redo most of it yourself.

Researchers have a name for this: workslop. It is one of the less advertised effects of putting AI tools on every desk, and it helps explain why many companies say they are using AI heavily without seeing the gains they expected.

## Key points

- Workslop is AI-generated work that looks complete but leaves the real thinking to whoever receives it.
- In a survey of 1,150 US workers, about 40% said they had received some in the previous month.
- Each incident took nearly two hours to deal with. The researchers put the cost at $186 per employee per month.
- The remedy is less about the tools than about how work is handed from one person to the next.

## Where the term comes from

The word was coined in 2025 by researchers at BetterUp Labs, a workplace research group, and the Stanford Social Media Lab. They defined workslop as AI-generated work content that masquerades as good work but lacks the substance to move a task forward.

The important part of the definition is who pays. A poor piece of work used to look poor. AI makes weak work look polished, so the problem is discovered later, by someone else, at a higher cost.

## What the research found

The team surveyed 1,150 full-time workers in the United States.

| Finding | Figure |
|---|---|
| Workers who received workslop in the previous month | About 40% |
| Share of the work content people receive that qualifies | 15.4% |
| Time spent dealing with each incident | Nearly two hours |
| Estimated cost per employee | $186 a month |
| Estimated cost for a company of 10,000 people | More than $9 million a year |

Most of it travels sideways. About 40% came from colleagues at the same level, 18% went from staff up to managers, and 16% came down from managers.

The cost is not only time. Respondents said receiving workslop left them annoyed (53%), confused (38%) or offended (22%), and 42% said they trusted the sender less afterwards.

Two cautions about the numbers. They come from what people reported about their own experience, not from measurement, and the dollar figure is an estimate built on those reports. One of the organisations behind the study also sells workplace coaching. The pattern is believable. The precise figures should be held loosely.

## Why it happens

- **Speed is rewarded.** A draft that appears in two minutes feels like productivity, and the person who produced it is not the one who discovers its faults.
- **Polish hides gaps.** Clean formatting and confident language are easy for AI and are the signals people have always used to judge whether work was done carefully.
- **Mandates without guidance.** Staff told to "use AI more" will use it for everything, including tasks where it adds nothing.
- **The checking is skipped.** AI output needs verifying. Under time pressure, that is the step that goes.

The researchers distinguish between two kinds of user. "Pilots" use AI deliberately to do better work. "Passengers" use it mainly to avoid work. The same tool produces very different results in the two cases.

## How to recognise it

- It is longer than the task needed.
- It restates the question at length before answering.
- Its facts and figures have no source, or do not survive a check.
- It is generic, with nothing that reflects your company, customer or situation.
- It ends without a recommendation or a next step.

## What to do about it

**If you are sending work**

1. Check every fact, figure and name before you share anything an AI helped produce.
2. Ask whether it does the job the recipient needs done, not merely whether it reads well.
3. Say what you did. A short note such as "drafted with AI, I have verified the numbers in section two but not the market data" saves the reader an hour.
4. Cut it down. If the answer is one paragraph, send one paragraph.

**If you are receiving it**

Point to the specific gap and propose what happens next. "Can we check the data before we use this?" works better than a general complaint, and keeps the exchange about the work.

**If you manage a team**

- **Measure the whole chain.** Count the total time the team spends until a piece of work is accepted, including corrections, and not how fast the first draft appeared.
- **Set a standard for handovers.** Every piece of work passed on should state the intended result, what has been checked, what is still open and who owns it.
- **Give guidance, not just permission.** The researchers advise against blanket instructions to use AI and in favour of clear rules about where it helps.
- **Train the subject as well as the tool.** Someone who cannot judge a good financial analysis will not get one out of a chatbot.
- **Keep a second reviewer** for anything with real consequences.

## Why this matters for Indian companies

India's IT services and outsourcing industry is built on delivering work to clients. AI tools promise to do that work faster, and clients are already asking for lower prices as a result.

Workslop is the risk in that bargain. If faster delivery means more errors reaching the client, the saving is lost in rework and in damaged trust. Firms that measure quality at the point of delivery, and not just speed of production, will be better placed.

For managers everywhere the lesson is the same one that emerged from C.H. Robinson's account of [using AI agents across its business](/tech/ch-robinson-ai-agents-logistics-productivity/): the technology is available to everyone, and the results depend on the discipline around it.

For employees, there is an opportunity. As polished-looking output becomes cheap, the people who can tell good work from plausible work, and who check before they send, become more valuable.
