How you think about getting older may affect how well you age. Research suggests that people with positive views of ageing tend to stay healthier and live longer than those who see it mainly as decline.

The World Health Organization's global report on ageism puts it simply: "Ageism can change how we view ourselves." Addressing it, the WHO argues, matters for health as well as for fairness.

## Key points

- In the UK, one in three people report experiencing prejudice or discrimination because of their age.
- In a US study of 2,000 adults aged 50 to 80, 93% said they experienced some form of everyday ageism, most often in the form of their own negative beliefs about ageing.
- Negative stereotypes about ageing are absorbed from childhood, as early as age three, according to one study.
- By 2030, one in six adults in the world will be 60 or older.

## How ageism harms health

Researchers describe two mechanisms.

**Stereotype embodiment.** Beliefs about ageing picked up from family, media and culture are gradually internalised. As people get older, those beliefs become self-fulfilling: someone who expects to decline may stop learning, exercising or socialising, and then declines.

**Stereotype threat.** People reminded of their age before a task tend to do worse at it, possibly because of the anxiety it creates, said Hannah Swift, a psychologist at the University of Kent, in comments to the BBC.

The best-known study in this area, by Becca Levy of Yale University, followed older adults in Ohio for more than two decades. Those with more positive views of their own ageing lived on average about seven and a half years longer than those with negative views, after accounting for factors such as health and income. Later studies have linked positive attitudes to better recovery from disability and lower risk of dementia.

Studies like these show association, and attitude is not a substitute for medical care. But the findings have been repeated often enough to be taken seriously.

## Language matters

Parminder Raina of McMaster University's Institute for Research on Aging criticises phrases such as "grey tsunami", which present an ageing population as a disaster. "Ageing is not a destructive force," he told the BBC. "It is actually a remarkable achievement of the modern public health system."

## Ageism at work

For business readers, ageism is also a workplace and economic issue.

- **Hiring.** Older candidates are often screened out as "overqualified" or assumed to be poor with technology.
- **Training.** Employers invest less in training older staff, which then makes them less current.
- **Talent loss.** As populations age, companies that push out experienced workers lose knowledge they cannot easily replace.
- **It runs both ways.** Younger workers face age stereotypes too, such as being labelled lazy or entitled.

Mixed-age teams, training open to all ages, and judging people on skills rather than birth year are practical remedies. In India, where the retirement age in many organisations is 58 to 60 and life expectancy is rising, many people now face two or more decades after formal work. How they spend them matters for their health and finances.

## What you can do

1. **Notice your own assumptions.** Catch thoughts like "I'm too old to learn this."
2. **Keep learning.** New skills, from a language to an app, protect confidence and the brain.
3. **Stay active and connected.** Physical activity and social contact are two of the strongest predictors of healthy ageing. 5. **Challenge ageist language** at home and at work, including jokes.
