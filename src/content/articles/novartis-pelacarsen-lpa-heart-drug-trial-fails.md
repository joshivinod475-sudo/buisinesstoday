A drug that did exactly what it was designed to do has failed its most important test.

Novartis's experimental medicine pelacarsen lowered a harmful blood particle called lipoprotein(a) by 78% in a seven-year trial of more than 8,000 heart patients. It made no difference to the number of heart attacks, strokes or deaths.

The full results were presented at the American Heart Association's annual meeting in Chicago on 7 November and published in The New England Journal of Medicine. The company has stopped developing the drug for heart disease. The outcome raises a hard question for the whole field, and offers a lesson that goes beyond one medicine.

## Key points

- Pelacarsen cut lipoprotein(a), known as Lp(a), by an average of 78%.
- Major heart events occurred in about 12% of patients in both the drug and placebo groups over nearly five years.
- Side effects were more common on the drug, and 19 patients had a severe allergic reaction.
- Three trials of rival Lp(a) drugs are still running. Researchers say patients in them should not drop out.

## What Lp(a) is

Lipoprotein(a) is a particle in the blood that carries cholesterol. Like LDL, the so-called bad cholesterol, it can contribute to the build-up of fatty deposits in arteries.

Three things set it apart:

- **It is inherited.** A person's level is set largely by their genes and stays much the same through life.
- **Lifestyle barely affects it.** Diet, exercise and statins have little effect.
- **It is common.** About one person in five worldwide, roughly 1.4 billion people, is thought to have a raised level, the American Heart Association says.

High levels are linked to more heart attacks and strokes. A level of 125 nmol/L or above is associated with about 1.4 times the long-term risk, and 250 nmol/L or above with at least double. There is no approved drug that lowers it.

That is why pelacarsen attracted so much attention. It was the first Lp(a)-lowering drug to complete a trial large enough to show whether reducing the particle actually protects the heart.

## What the trial tested

The study, called Lp(a) HORIZON, was paid for by Novartis and ran from 2019 to 2026 at 797 sites in 42 countries.

- **Who took part:** 8,323 adults who already had cardiovascular disease and a high Lp(a) level. Four in five had previously had a heart attack.
- **What they received:** a monthly injection of pelacarsen or a placebo, on top of their usual treatment. Most were already on high-dose statins.
- **What was measured:** the time to a first major event, meaning a heart attack, stroke, urgent procedure to open an artery, or death from a cardiovascular cause.

## What it found

| Measure | Pelacarsen | Placebo |
|---|---|---|
| Change in Lp(a) | Down about 78% | Little change |
| Major cardiovascular events | About 12.3% | About 12.1% |
| Serious adverse events | 37.3% | 41.1% |
| Stopped treatment because of side effects | About 21% | About 7% |
| Severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) | 19 patients | None |

The event rates, reported by the trade press, cover a typical follow-up of 4.7 years. There was no benefit even among patients with the highest Lp(a) levels.

Flu-like symptoms, reactions at the injection site and allergic reactions were all more common on the drug. No patient died from an allergic reaction.

## Why it failed

Nobody yet knows. "This will leave a lot of people scratching their heads," the trial's leader told the health news site STAT.

Dr Steven Nissen of the Cleveland Clinic, the study's senior author, called the result unexpected. He stressed that it does not prove Lp(a) is harmless.

Researchers are considering several explanations:

- **The patients were already well treated.** Three-quarters had their blood pressure and cholesterol at recommended targets. There may have been little risk left to remove.
- **It may be too late by then.** Lp(a) acts over a lifetime. Lowering it after decades of exposure, in people who already have heart disease, may not undo the damage.
- **This drug may not have gone far enough.** Newer medicines reduce Lp(a) by more than 78%.
- **The link may be weaker than thought.** A particle can be a marker of risk without being its cause.

The trial has limits too. Some events happened during the Covid-19 pandemic. And 78% of participants were white, with fewer than 4% of African ancestry, a group whose Lp(a) levels are about twice as high on average.

## What it means for the industry

For Novartis the result ends a programme it took on in 2019, when it licensed the drug from the US biotechnology company Ionis. The company said it would discontinue development for cardiovascular disease, including studies still under way.

For competitors, the reading is more mixed. Amgen, Eli Lilly and others have Lp(a) drugs in large trials. Analysts quoted by BioSpace and Scrip called the result a setback for all of them, since they rest on the same idea. Those drugs work differently and lower Lp(a) further, so their trials could still succeed. Nissen urged patients enrolled in them to stay in.

## The lesson: a better number is not a better outcome

This is the part that matters beyond cardiology.

Doctors and investors often judge a drug by what it does to a measurement: a cholesterol level, a blood sugar reading, a tumour's size. These are called surrogate markers, and they are useful because they change quickly. Outcomes that patients care about, such as living longer or avoiding a heart attack, take years to measure.

Usually the two move together. Sometimes they do not. Pelacarsen improved the number about as much as anyone hoped and changed nothing that mattered to patients.

For anyone who follows pharmaceutical shares, it is a reminder to ask what a trial actually measured. An early study showing that a drug "lowers" something is a reason for a larger trial. It is not proof the drug works. That is worth remembering when [reading a company's results announcement](/markets/read-quarterly-results-in-five-minutes/), where early-stage data is often presented in its best light.

## What it means for patients, including in India

Studies suggest raised Lp(a) is common among South Asians, and cardiologists in India increasingly test for it, particularly in people with heart disease at a young age or a strong family history. The test is a simple blood test and usually needs to be done only once.

The failed trial does not change the practical advice:

- **Testing is still worthwhile.** Nissen said so directly. A high level usually causes no symptoms, and knowing about it helps a doctor judge overall risk.
- **Treat what can be treated.** With no drug for Lp(a), the approach is to control everything else firmly: LDL cholesterol, blood pressure, diabetes, smoking and weight.
- **Do not chase unproven remedies.** No supplement has been shown to lower Lp(a) and prevent heart attacks.
