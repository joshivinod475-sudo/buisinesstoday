Seven Democratic senators have asked the US Federal Trade Commission to investigate whether large companies are breaking the law by keeping tariff refunds instead of passing them on to customers who paid higher prices.

In a letter to FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson dated 11 November, the senators argued that companies which raised prices "ostensibly due to tariff costs" and then refuse to refund consumers may be violating the FTC Act, which bans unfair and deceptive business practices.

## Key points

- The Supreme Court ruled in February 2026 that tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) were not authorised by that law.
- Refunds of those duties go to the importer of record, usually the company that brought goods into the US, not to the shoppers who paid higher prices.
- Some companies, including DHL, FedEx and UPS, have set up systems to pass refunds on. Many others have not said what they will do.
- More than 80 proposed class actions have been filed against companies by customers seeking a share of the refunds.

## How tariff refunds work

When the US imposes a tariff, the importing company pays it to US Customs and Border Protection. Many companies then raise prices to cover the cost, passing part or all of it on to customers.

When a court rules that a tariff was unlawful, the government must return the money. Under US customs law, it goes to the importer, which is the only party that dealt with customs. Shoppers have no direct claim on it.

The result is a possible windfall. A company that raised prices to cover tariffs, and then gets the tariffs back, may end up having been paid twice.

## What the senators want

The letter asks the FTC to examine whether companies:

- told customers that price rises were caused by tariffs;
- have received, or will receive, refunds of those tariffs; and
- are keeping the refunds without lowering prices or compensating customers.

If a company blamed tariffs for a price rise, the argument goes, keeping the refund could amount to a deceptive practice. The FTC has not announced an investigation. It is led by a Republican chairman, so the request may carry less weight before Democrats take control of Congress in January.

## The lawsuits

Customers are also going to court. Law firms report more than 80 proposed class actions so far, across retail, consumer goods and logistics. Most argue unjust enrichment: that the companies have been enriched at their customers' expense. These cases face a hard question. Proving how much of a price rise was caused by a tariff, and who paid it, is difficult when prices change for many reasons.

## What it means for businesses

**Exporters, including Indian ones, are generally not entitled.** Refunds go to the importer of record. An Indian exporter that sold goods to a US importer will usually not receive a refund unless it was itself the importer, or its contract says otherwise.

**Check contracts.** Some supply contracts include tariff-sharing clauses. Businesses that absorbed part of the cost should check whether they can claim a share.

**Be careful with "tariff surcharge" messaging.** Companies that labelled surcharges as tariff costs may face more pressure to refund them.
