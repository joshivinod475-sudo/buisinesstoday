The US Senate has passed a bipartisan bill to speed up approvals for energy and infrastructure projects, including power lines, clean energy plants and data centres, USA Today reported on 19 November. It is one of the most significant pieces of energy legislation in years and the first major bill the Senate has taken up since the November midterm elections.

Supporters say it will help the US meet soaring electricity demand from AI. Environmental groups say it weakens protections and favours fossil fuels. The bill must still be reconciled with the House of Representatives before it can become law.

## Key points

- The bill cleared a key procedural vote on 9 November by 73 votes to 25, a sign of broad bipartisan support.
- It aims to shorten the time needed to approve energy projects of all kinds, from solar and wind to gas pipelines.
- It would require data centres to cover the cost of the transmission lines they need, so households and other businesses do not pay.
- Both the American Clean Power Association and the American Petroleum Institute support it. President Trump urged senators to pass it.
- The House passed its own permitting bill, the SPEED Act, in December 2025, so the two versions must be combined.

## Why permitting matters

In the US, large energy projects can take years to win approval under environmental review laws and through court challenges. New transmission lines are especially slow, often taking a decade or more. Meanwhile, electricity demand is rising for the first time in decades because of data centres, factories and electrification.

Slow permitting means:

- **higher power prices**, as demand outpaces new supply
- **delayed clean energy**, since many solar and wind projects wait years for grid connections
- **pressure on the grid**, raising the risk of shortages during peak demand

## What the bill would do

| Area | Change |
|---|---|
| Environmental reviews | Tighter deadlines and page limits for federal reviews |
| Legal challenges | Shorter windows to file lawsuits against approved projects |
| Transmission | Easier approval of interstate power lines |
| Data centres | Data centres to pay for associated transmission costs |
| Fossil fuels | Faster approvals for gas pipelines and related infrastructure; critics say this includes LNG export facilities |

## Who supports it and who opposes it

**Supporters** include clean energy and oil and gas industry groups, which rarely agree. Both want faster approvals. Lawmakers argue the bill will help the US compete in AI and keep electricity affordable.

**Opponents** include major environmental groups. They say the bill could weaken clean water and endangered species protections, limit the public's ability to challenge projects in court and enable a build-out of gas-fired power for data centres.

## What happens next

The Senate and House versions differ, and both chambers must agree on identical text before the bill goes to the president. House Democratic leaders have said the Senate bill falls short in some areas, and some Republicans may want changes too. The current Congress ends in early January, after which Democrats take control of both chambers, which could change the negotiations if no deal is reached before then.

## What it means for data centres and AI

Data centres are at the centre of the debate. The International Energy Agency expects [their electricity use to roughly double by 2030](/world/data-centre-electricity-demand-iea-2030-grids/). Faster permitting could help connect new power supplies, while the requirement to pay for transmission responds to voters' anger about rising bills, an issue that featured in the [Texas elections](/world/texas-election-data-centre-boom-democrats-ai-moratorium/) and in [debates over taxing data centres](/economy/ai-data-centre-tax-debate-trump-democrats/).

## Lessons for India

India faces similar problems on a different scale. Transmission lines needed to carry renewable power from Rajasthan and Gujarat often face delays over land acquisition and right-of-way. The [Ministry of Power](https://powermin.gov.in/) has introduced compensation reforms to speed up such projects. As India's own data centre industry grows, who pays for new grid capacity will become a major policy question.
