US government borrowing costs edged higher on Thursday, with the yield on the 10-year Treasury note at about 5.09%, as investors weighed new economic data and minutes from the Federal Reserve's October meeting that pointed towards an interest rate rise in December.

Traders now put the chance of a quarter-point rate rise at the Fed's 8–9 December meeting at 95%, according to CME Group's FedWatch tool, CNBC reported.

## Key points

- 10-year yield: about 5.085%. 30-year: 5.426%. 2-year: 4.826%.
- Fed minutes showed officials confident about raising rates in December but waiting for more data.
- Officials discussed AI as "a material source of inflation risk".
- Fed Governor Christopher Waller said a half-point rise was likely needed and that the Fed may need to "shock people".
- Weekly jobless claims rose to 203,000, above the 197,000 expected.

## Why the Fed is talking about raising rates

Inflation has picked up again in 2026, driven by higher fuel prices after the Iran war, tariffs and strong spending on AI infrastructure. Consumer prices rose 0.4% in October alone.

The Fed cut rates by three-quarters of a percentage point last year. Waller, who supported those cuts, told CNBC the Fed might now need to reverse them "and do more". "I'm very, very concerned about inflation. It's kind of headed in the wrong direction, and it's spreading out," he said.

## Why AI is an inflation risk

It may seem odd that a technology meant to raise productivity is seen as inflationary. The concern is the spending to build it. Companies are investing close to $1 trillion a year in data centres, chips and power. That demand pushes up prices for electricity, construction, equipment and skilled labour before any productivity gains arrive.

The latest data shows both sides. US [productivity grew 3.6% in the third quarter](/economy/us-productivity-q3-2026-ai-boom-workers/), but the [International Energy Agency warns that data centres could double their electricity use by 2030](/world/data-centre-electricity-demand-iea-2030-grids/), straining power grids and bills.

## What higher US yields mean for India

**Foreign investment flows.** When US government bonds pay over 5% with little risk, global investors have less reason to buy emerging market assets. That can pull money out of Indian shares and bonds.

**The rupee.** Higher US rates tend to strengthen the dollar and put pressure on the rupee, raising the cost of imports such as oil.

**The RBI's choices.** The Reserve Bank of India sets rates for Indian conditions, but large gaps with US rates can weigh on the rupee. Our explainer on [how the RBI repo rate affects your EMI](/markets/repo-rate-and-your-emi/) shows how rate decisions reach borrowers.

**For Indians with US exposure,** higher yields mean better returns on new dollar deposits and bonds, but losses on existing long-term bonds and pressure on high-valuation growth stocks, including the newly listed [Anthropic](/markets/anthropic-ipo-prices-83-record-81-billion/).

## What to watch

Revised US productivity data and November inflation figures before the Fed's 8–9 December meeting. The key question is whether the Fed raises by a quarter point, as markets expect, or by the half point Waller has suggested. The meeting will also come amid the legal fight over [Fed Governor Lisa Cook](/economy/lisa-cook-fed-white-house-hearing-fed-independence/).
