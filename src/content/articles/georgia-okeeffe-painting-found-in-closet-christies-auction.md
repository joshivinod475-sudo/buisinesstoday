A painting by Georgia O'Keeffe that was bought for $3,800 in 1963 and spent about four decades in a box in a New York closet is due to be sold at Christie's on Tuesday, 17 November. The auction house estimates it will fetch between $3 million and $5 million.

Until it was unpacked this year, the picture was known to scholars only from a photograph taken in 1931.

## Key points

- The painting is a 1930 landscape of Lobo Peak, near Taos in New Mexico.
- Christie's believes it is the first of three related works. One of the others is in the Metropolitan Museum of Art.
- It was found when a New York apartment was cleared after the death of its owner, a woman in her late nineties.
- The record for an O'Keeffe at auction is $44.4 million, set in 2014.

## How it was lost and found

A New York gallery sold the painting in 1963 to a woman who gave it to her daughter, the family believes as a present for completing a master's degree, the Associated Press reported.

The daughter hung it in her apartment in Manhattan's East Village for years. In the mid-1980s she noticed a guest examining it too closely. She put it back in its original crate and stored it in a closet, behind coats and boxes.

She told a niece about it around 2022. It was rediscovered this year when the apartment was being cleared. The family has asked not to be identified.

According to the AP, the owner had worked for the US Foreign Service and collected antiques and abstract art. Nothing else she owned compared with the O'Keeffe.

## Why it is valued so highly

**The date.** O'Keeffe first worked in New Mexico in 1929. A picture from 1930 comes from the start of the period for which she is best known. Tylee Abbott, head of American art at Christie's, called it "particularly important because of its early date".

**The series.** Christie's believes it was the first of three paintings of the same mountain. The second is in the Metropolitan Museum and the third was owned for many years by the Rockefeller family.

**The proof.** The back carries O'Keeffe's mark, a star with the initials "OK" inside it, and the picture matches the 1931 photograph. There is also a continuous record of ownership from the gallery sale in 1963. In the art market, that record, called provenance, is often worth as much as the object.

**The condition.** A painting kept in the dark in its crate has been protected from light and handling.

## What the numbers mean

If the painting sells within its estimate, the return on the 1963 price works out at roughly 11% to 12% a year over 63 years, before any costs.

That figure flatters art as an investment, for four reasons.

- **Survivorship.** This is the picture that turned out to be valuable. Most art bought in 1963 is worth little today, and nobody writes about it.
- **Costs.** The seller pays the auction house a commission, and the buyer pays a premium on top of the hammer price. Insurance, storage and restoration cost money every year.
- **Tax.** Profits on collectibles are taxed, in the US at a higher rate than shares.
- **No income and no easy exit.** A painting pays no dividend, and it can take months to sell at a fair price.

An estimate is also only an estimate. Works fail to sell, and others go for several times the top figure. O'Keeffe's record, for "Jimson Weed/White Flower No. 1" at Sotheby's in 2014, was almost three times the high estimate.

## If you find something valuable at home

Families in India often hold old paintings, jewellery, coins or furniture without knowing what they are worth. The story suggests some practical steps.

1. **Do not clean or restore it.** Amateur cleaning destroys value.
2. **Keep every piece of paper.** Receipts, letters, old photographs showing the object and gallery labels on the back all help establish provenance.
3. **Get more than one opinion.** Ask an established auction house and an independent valuer. Be wary of anyone who offers to buy on the spot.
4. **Check the law before selling abroad.** In India, objects more than 100 years old are covered by the Antiquities and Art Treasures Act. Many must be registered, and they cannot be exported privately. Works by certain artists classed as national art treasures cannot be exported either.
5. **Insure it** once you know what it is.
