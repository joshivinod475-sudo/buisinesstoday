Nearly one million young people in the UK are not in employment, education or training, a group known as Neets. A government-commissioned review led by the former minister Alan Milburn on how to bring that number down was due to be published on Tuesday, 17 November.

The issue is not unique to Britain. Every economy that loses young people between school and work pays for it twice: in lost output now and in weaker earnings and health for decades.

## Key points

- School absence in England has fallen since the pandemic but remains well above pre-Covid levels.
- 168,000 pupils, about 2.3%, missed more than half their lessons in the autumn and spring terms of 2025-26. In spring 2019 the figure was under 60,000.
- Research links persistent absence to much higher rates of being Neet between 16 and 24.
- The think tank the Centre for Social Justice estimates that 133,000 of today's persistently absent GCSE pupils are on course to spend at least a year as Neet.

## Why children stop going to school

The BBC spoke to a 13-year-old autistic girl who had not attended school for 15 months after struggling with the move to secondary school. She described getting stuck at the front door each morning, and feeling ill in the car park on days she got that far. She now attends an alternative school with smaller classes.

Her case points to a shift in the debate. Absence is no longer seen mainly as truancy. Anxiety, mental health problems and special educational needs that schools are not equipped to meet are a growing share. "We're not lazy," she told the BBC. "We want to go… but we can't."

Others argue the expectation of daily attendance itself has weakened since the pandemic. Dan Lilley of the Centre for Social Justice said ministers must restore "the expectation that children are in school every day", calling a return to school "the first step to getting young people into work".

The two views are not exclusive, and the solutions differ for each group.

## Why it is an economic problem

- **Lost output.** Young people outside work and training produce nothing and pay no tax, while often drawing benefits.
- **Scarring.** Long spells out of work early in life are linked to lower earnings and higher unemployment years later.
- **Health.** Long-term worklessness is linked to poorer mental and physical health, which adds to public costs.
- **Labour shortages.** Britain, like many ageing economies, needs more workers. A million young people outside the labour market is a large untapped supply.

## The question for India

India measures the same thing. Government surveys have put the share of young Indians not in employment, education or training at roughly a quarter or more, among the highest in large economies, with a far higher rate among young women than young men. The figures depend on the survey and year, so treat any single number with care.

The causes differ from Britain's. In India they include the end of schooling for many girls at marriage, a shortage of formal jobs that match qualifications, and young graduates waiting for government or white-collar posts rather than taking informal work.

India's demographic advantage, a large working-age population, only pays off if those young people are working or learning. Three lessons from the UK debate apply:

1. **Track absence early.** Missing school is the first sign of later disengagement, and it is measurable.
2. **Do not treat all absence the same.** Anxiety, disability, family duties and lack of interest need different responses.
3. **Build bridges to work.** Apprenticeships and vocational routes keep young people attached to the labour market.
