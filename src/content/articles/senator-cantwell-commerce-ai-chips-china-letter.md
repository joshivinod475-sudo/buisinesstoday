A senior US senator has accused the government department responsible for export controls of failing to stop advanced artificial intelligence chips from reaching China.

In a letter dated 15 November to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, Senator Maria Cantwell said the department had "delayed action on repeated national security warnings". Cantwell is the senior Democrat on the Senate committee that oversees the department. Reuters reported the letter on Monday.

A spokesperson for Lutnick declined to comment on it.

## Key points

- Cantwell says the Commerce Department failed to use its powers and that this "undermined America's national security".
- She says the department suspended an inquiry into Megaspeed, a Singapore-based company. Megaspeed says its business is lawful.
- She argues that a 25% government fee on certain chip sales to China creates "a perverse financial incentive" to approve exports.
- The letter adds to pressure in Congress for tighter rules after the resignation of Nvidia's chief executive.

## What the letter says

Cantwell's central charge is about enforcement. "America cannot maintain its leadership in AI if the agency responsible for protecting that advantage fails to enforce its own controls," she wrote.

She makes three specific points, according to Reuters.

**Delay.** The department, she says, did not act on repeated warnings from other parts of the government.

**The Megaspeed inquiry.** Cantwell says Commerce suspended a probe into Megaspeed. US authorities have been examining whether the company helped Chinese customers obtain Nvidia chips that are subject to export controls, The New York Times reported in October 2025.

**Conflicts.** She says financial conflicts raise "serious questions" about whether business interests were put ahead of national security, and she singles out the fee the administration collects on some chip exports.

## What Megaspeed says

The company has not been found to have broken any rules, Reuters noted. Megaspeed has said it conducts a "legitimate and lawful business" and that media claims about it are not supported by verifiable evidence. China denies the allegations.

The company is at the centre of the events that led to Jensen Huang's resignation from Nvidia on 12 November. Reports have linked him to a former Megaspeed executive whom US officials are said to suspect of working for Chinese intelligence. That claim is unproven. Megaspeed says the person left the company in August 2025.

## The 25% fee, explained

In December 2025, President Trump allowed Nvidia to sell its H200 chip, an advanced model that is not its most powerful, to approved customers in China. Under the arrangement the US government receives 25% of the revenue from those sales.

Supporters see it as a way to earn money from trade that would otherwise be lost while keeping the most advanced chips out of China.

Cantwell's objection is that it gives the government a financial stake in approving more exports. The department deciding whether a sale is safe is part of a government that profits when the answer is yes.

## The split inside the administration

The letter draws on an unusual public disagreement between cabinet members.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has said that the Treasury, the Pentagon, the State Department and the US Trade Representative's office had all favoured placing Megaspeed on the government's trade blacklist for months. The Commerce Department, which controls the list, had not done so. We cover his remarks in our report on [Bessent's call for a shake-up at Nvidia](/world/bessent-nvidia-shakeup-lutnick-megaspeed/).

## Where Congress is heading

Cantwell is not alone, and the criticism is not confined to one party.

- House Speaker Mike Johnson, a Republican, said last week that there is growing agreement in Congress in favour of new restrictions.
- Senators Elizabeth Warren, a Democrat, and Jim Banks, a Republican, argue that export decisions with major security implications should face more congressional oversight.
- Several proposals already under discussion would let Congress review licences for chip sales to China, as we described in our report on [the US-China chip fight after Huang's exit](/tech/nvidia-jensen-huang-resignation-us-china-ai-chip-rivalry/).

From January, Democrats will control both chambers. Committee chairs will then be able to demand documents and call officials to testify.

## Why it matters outside the US

**For chip buyers everywhere.** Tighter enforcement usually means more paperwork for all customers, including checks on who the final user of a chip is and where it is installed. Indian data-centre operators and cloud companies buying Nvidia hardware should expect closer scrutiny of their orders.

**For countries in between.** The Megaspeed case concerns a company in Singapore. It shows that Washington is looking at third countries through which chips might be redirected. India's interest lies in being seen as a trusted destination, which matters for its ambitions in AI computing and semiconductor manufacturing.

**For the US-China talks.** President Trump is due to meet Xi Jinping in Shenzhen this week. Chips are on the agenda, and the pressure from Congress narrows his room to offer concessions. We cover the trip in our report on [Trump's visit to Asia](/world/trump-asia-trip-quad-apec-xi-putin/).
