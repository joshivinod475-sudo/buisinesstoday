Two very different stories pulled US fintech shares in opposite directions this week. Coinbase, the largest American crypto exchange, received final approval to run a federally chartered trust bank. At the same time, a third straight month of faster-than-expected inflation pushed investors to prepare for a US interest rate hike in December, which hurt companies that make their money from lending.

Here is what happened, and why it matters beyond Wall Street.

## Key points

- Coinbase said on 9 November that the US Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) had given final approval for Coinbase National Trust Company.
- The charter covers custody, meaning safekeeping of digital assets. It does not let Coinbase take deposits or make loans.
- US consumer prices rose 0.4% in October, above the 0.3% economists expected.
- Lenders SoFi and Affirm fell for a second week. Card networks Mastercard and Visa rose for a sixth.

## What Coinbase's trust charter actually means

A national trust charter puts a company under the direct supervision of the OCC, the federal agency that regulates national banks in the US. Until now, crypto custody firms have mostly operated under licences from individual states.

For Coinbase, the practical gain is credibility with large institutions. Pension funds, asset managers and the issuers of crypto exchange-traded funds need a custodian to hold assets on their behalf, and many prefer or are required to use a federally supervised one. Coinbase already acts as custodian for several US spot Bitcoin funds.

The limits matter as much as the permissions. Coinbase National Trust Company can hold digital assets for clients in a fiduciary capacity. It cannot accept deposits or lend. It is a trust bank and not a full commercial bank.

The approval came in two steps. The OCC granted preliminary conditional approval on 2 April this year, and its records list 6 November as the date the charter took effect. Coinbase is not the first: Circle, which issues the USDC stablecoin, received final approval in July, and Ripple followed in October, according to reports by Decrypt and others.

## Not everyone is happy

The charters have drawn criticism from traditional banks and some lawmakers.

The Independent Community Bankers of America, a trade group for small US banks, has sued the OCC over the rules behind these approvals. Senator Elizabeth Warren challenged at least nine crypto trust charter decisions, including Coinbase's conditional approval, in a letter to the regulator on 18 May.

Their argument is that crypto firms are getting the standing of a federal bank charter without the full set of obligations that deposit-taking banks carry. The lawsuit has not been decided, so the legal footing of these charters could still be tested.

## Inflation came in hot again

The bigger market mover was the US inflation report. The [Consumer Price Index](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.htm) rose 0.4% in October from September. That was slower than the previous month's 0.6% but above forecasts. Core prices, which leave out food and energy, rose 0.3% for a third month running.

There was an unusual gap in the report. The Bureau of Labor Statistics could not publish a year-on-year inflation rate because price data was not collected in October 2025, during the US government shutdown. Private estimates put annual inflation at roughly 3.8% to 4%.

Federal Reserve officials responded firmly. Governor Christopher Waller said a half-percentage-point increase should be considered if inflation keeps its momentum, and regional Fed presidents Lorie Logan and Austan Goolsbee made similar points during the week. The Fed's next decision is due on 9 December.

## Why lenders fell and card networks rose

Higher interest rates do not hit all fintech companies the same way.

**Lenders feel it first.** Companies such as SoFi Technologies and Affirm borrow money to lend it on. When rates rise, their funding gets more expensive and customers borrow less. Both stocks fell for a second straight week.

**Payment networks are more insulated.** Mastercard and Visa earn a small fee on every transaction and carry no lending risk, so their income tends to rise with prices. Both gained for a sixth week. Citi raised its price target on Mastercard to $800 from $675 and on Visa to $490, while Morgan Stanley lifted its Visa target to $471, according to analyst notes reported by Stocktwits. Price targets are opinions about the future and are often wrong.

The same logic applies in India. When the Reserve Bank of India changes its policy rate, [loan EMIs move with the repo rate](/articles/repo-rate-and-your-emi/), and lenders feel the change well before payment companies do.

## Other fintech news this week

- **Robinhood** reported October figures on 12 November: 28.9 million funded customers, $15.1 billion in net deposits for the month, and a 136% rise in event-contract trading volume from a year earlier.
- **PayPal** announced a tie-up with Workday on 12 November that lets employees at participating companies route their salary into PayPal and get paid early. Its Venmo unit launched a receipt-scanning tool for splitting bills on 9 November.
- **FINRA**, the US brokerage industry's self-regulator, proposed shrinking its board from 25 seats to 20 and ending the majority that public members have held since 1996. The plan, filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission, would give industry representatives at least seven seats and public members at least five.

## What it means for Indian readers

**Crypto rules remain very different in India.** There is no equivalent of a trust charter here. Gains on virtual digital assets are taxed at a flat 30%, a 1% tax is deducted at source on transfers, and the Reserve Bank of India has repeatedly voiced concern about private cryptocurrencies. A US charter for Coinbase does not change any of that for Indian investors.

**A US rate hike travels.** Higher American interest rates tend to strengthen the dollar and can pull foreign money out of emerging markets, including India. That can weigh on the rupee and on Indian shares, at least in the short term.

**The payments contrast is worth noting.** Mastercard and Visa are valued for the fees they collect on each swipe. India's own system works differently, as our explainer on [what happens when you pay with UPI](/articles/what-happens-when-you-pay-with-upi/) shows, with no charge to the customer for most payments.
