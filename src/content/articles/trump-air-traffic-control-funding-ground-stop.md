President Donald Trump has made his first public comments on the nationwide ground stop that halted every US departure on Friday, and used them to demand more money from Congress.

Writing on his social media platform on Sunday, 15 November, Trump said his administration had begun upgrading the air traffic control system but that the work should not be done in "drips and drabs".

"If Congress approves the desperately needed money for Air Traffic Control, we can get it built quickly and efficiently," he wrote, calling much of the system, which he said was 50 years old, "RIDICULOUS!"

In a second post he blamed his predecessor, saying the Biden administration had left an "old and broken system" in worse condition than it found it.

## Key points

- Trump had said little about the ground stop for two days after it began on Friday afternoon.
- Congress approved $12.5 billion for the FAA last year. The administration says it needs about $30 billion more.
- Officials say the failure happened while a contractor was replacing old cabling. The exact cause is still under investigation.
- The request lands as Democrats prepare to take control of Congress in January.

## How much has been spent, and how much is being asked for

| Money | Amount | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Biden's 2021 infrastructure law | $25 billion for the FAA, covering airports, towers and the foundations of air traffic control | Approved in 2021 |
| Republican tax and spending law | $12.5 billion for the air traffic control overhaul | Approved in 2025 |
| New request | About $30 billion more | Not yet approved |

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy has called the $12.5 billion a down payment. He says all of it is already committed. With that money the FAA has replaced thousands of copper connections with fibre-optic lines and upgraded hundreds of radio systems, according to the Associated Press.

The size of the new request varies in reports. Most put it at about $30 billion, the figure Duffy gave in a letter to Congress the day before the ground stop. At least one outlet has reported a figure of $50 billion.

## What the administration says went wrong

Duffy was the government's main voice on Sunday's political talk shows. His account is that the contractor L3Harris Technologies was replacing copper wire with fibre when the network became overloaded, and that the problem spread from a facility in Texas across the country.

He said the administration had not expected the failure. On responsibility, he told NBC that he could not apologise for what had been handed down to him, and that underinvestment went back 20 to 30 years under both parties.

L3Harris has said its routers "did not fail" and that it is helping with the investigation. No final finding has been published.

## The political problem

Three things complicate the request.

**The failure happened during the upgrade.** The administration's argument is that old equipment must be replaced. Critics will point out that the outage occurred while replacement work was under way, which raises questions about how the project is being managed as well as how it is funded.

**The blame is shared.** Trump has pointed at Biden. Duffy has pointed at decades of neglect, at the contractor and at Congress. Democrats have criticised him for blaming lawmakers who approved $12.5 billion only last year.

**Congress is about to change hands.** Democrats won majorities in both chambers in this month's elections and take over in January, as we explained in our report on [what a Democratic Congress means](/world/abdul-el-sayed-michigan-senate-win-us-midterms-2026/). Any large spending bill will then need their agreement, and they are likely to attach conditions and hold hearings.

There is common ground. Neither party disputes that the system is old, and a nationwide shutdown shortly before the Thanksgiving holiday is the kind of event that tends to move money through Congress.

## Why this matters outside the US

The US runs the busiest airspace in the world, and delays there spread to international routes within hours. Airlines flying between India and the US had to adjust schedules over the weekend.

The larger lesson applies everywhere. Air traffic systems, power grids and payment networks are built up over decades, and replacing them while they are in use is risky and expensive. Governments tend to postpone the spending until something fails. India has faced its own version: a fault in the messaging system at Delhi's air traffic control delayed hundreds of flights in November 2025.
