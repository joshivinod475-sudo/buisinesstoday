Customers of several of the largest banks in the United States reported trouble reaching their accounts and moving money on Monday, 16 November. JPMorgan Chase, the country's biggest bank, said a software bug in its own systems was responsible and that it was not a cyberattack.

Chase said customers kept access to their funds throughout. By Monday evening it said transactions were posting normally.

## Key points

- Reports of problems began on Monday morning and rose sharply in the afternoon, US time.
- Chase had the most complaints: nearly 4,800 on the tracking site Downdetector by 2 pm Eastern time, CBS News reported.
- Customers of Bank of America, U.S. Bank, Wells Fargo, Capital One and Truist also reported problems, as did users of the payment service Zelle.
- Chase said the fault was "a software bug in on-premise hardware systems". The other banks said their own systems were working.

## What each bank said

| Bank or service | What it said |
|---|---|
| Chase | A technical issue made some transactions slower. Customers "still have access to their accounts and funds". |
| Bank of America | Its "systems have been operating normally today". |
| U.S. Bank | Its systems were not directly affected, but Zelle payments involving other banks "may experience transaction delays". |
| Capital One | Not affected and operating normally. |
| Zelle | The service was "operating as it should". Delays could come from problems at a user's bank. |

Wells Fargo customers reported trouble signing in and completing transactions. CBS News's report did not include a statement from the bank.

Downdetector figures are reports sent in by users. They show where people noticed a problem. They do not measure how many customers were affected.

## How one bank's fault reached other banks' customers

Chase said in a social media post that the bug affected other institutions "when we were party to a transaction involving other banks", and that the problem was not systemic.

That explains the pattern. Banks do not work in isolation. A transfer from one bank to another passes through shared networks, and a payment needs both ends to respond. If one large bank is slow, a customer of a different bank sending money to it will see a delay in their own app, and will reasonably conclude that their own bank is down.

Zelle is a good example. It is run by Early Warning Services, a company owned by seven banks including Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo and U.S. Bank.

## The fourth large failure in five days

The bank problem followed a run of unrelated technology failures in the US: a [Microsoft outage that hit Outlook and Teams](/tech/microsoft-365-global-outage-restored/), a network fault that led to a [nationwide halt to flights](/world/us-flights-cancelled-nationwide-ground-stop-recovery/), and a [separate outage at United Airlines](/world/united-airlines-outage-weight-system-flights-delayed/) on the same Monday.

None has been linked to an attack, and nothing connects them. Mike Chapple of the University of Notre Dame told CBS News that what they share is "the complexity and fragility of the systems that we depend on every day". ## What customers should do in an outage

Experts quoted by CBS News gave practical advice that applies to any bank, in any country.

1. **Use another channel.** If the app fails, try a cash machine, a card payment, phone banking or a branch.
2. **Keep evidence.** Take a screenshot of any failed transaction, with the time. It helps if a payment has to be traced or a late fee disputed.
3. **Do not repeat a payment that may have gone through.** Wait and check, or you may pay twice.
4. **Ask for fees to be reversed** if a bill was paid late because the bank's system was down.
5. **Be suspicious of messages about the outage.** Craig Timm of the anti-financial-crime body ACAMS said consumers should "assume they will be targeted" by fake bank alerts, texts and offers of help on social media. A bank will not ask for a password or a one-time code by message.

## Why it matters in India

India's payment system is built the same way. A UPI payment passes from the payer's app to their bank, through the National Payments Corporation of India's network and on to the recipient's bank, as we explain in our guide to [what happens when you pay with UPI](/tech/what-happens-when-you-pay-with-upi/). A fault at one large bank shows up as failed payments in apps that have nothing wrong with them.

The same habits apply: keep a second way to pay, wait before retrying a payment that shows as pending, and ignore unexpected calls and messages offering to "fix" a failed transaction.
