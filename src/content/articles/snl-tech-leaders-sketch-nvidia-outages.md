A week that began with a scandal at Nvidia and went on to include a global Microsoft outage and a nationwide halt to US flights ended, predictably, as television comedy.

This weekend's edition of Saturday Night Live, the long-running American sketch show, opened fire on the technology industry's best-known figures. The host, comedian John Mulaney, played a CIA officer briefing a room of tech executives on why they should be suspicious when a stranger suddenly takes a romantic interest in them, TechCrunch reported.

The premise came straight from the news. Nvidia's Jensen Huang resigned on 12 November after reports of a relationship with a woman whom US officials are said to suspect of working for Chinese intelligence. That allegation is unproven and Beijing denies it.

## What the sketch showed

Cast members appeared as six of the industry's most recognisable leaders: Peter Thiel, OpenAI's Sam Altman, Anthropic's Dario Amodei, Elon Musk, Meta's Mark Zuckerberg and, arriving later, Huang himself.

The sharpest exchange was about reliability and not romance. When the Thiel character argues that technology leaders deserve more respect and demands an example of technology letting people down, the CIA officer points to Friday's grounding of flights across the United States. Asked for a second, he cites Thursday's Microsoft Outlook outage. Asked for a third, he refuses to continue.

The closing joke turned on the idea that this particular group of people is an unlikely one to have ended up holding so much of the world's wealth and power.

A sketch is satire, and nothing in it is a statement of fact about the real people portrayed.

## Why a comedy sketch is worth noticing

Mainstream comedy is a rough but useful gauge of public mood. A joke only works on a mass audience if the audience already knows the story. That a network show could build a whole sketch on chip export controls, a software outage and an air traffic failure suggests how far those subjects have travelled beyond the business pages.

It also shows which criticisms have stuck. Three themes ran through the sketch:

- **Concentration of power.** A handful of individuals now control companies that much of the economy depends on.
- **Fragility.** Systems promoted as the future failed twice in one week in ways that stopped ordinary people working and travelling. We examined that in our analysis of [whether everything is breaking](/tech/is-everything-broken-outages-week/).
- **Judgement.** The Nvidia affair has raised questions about how carefully some leaders guard information that governments consider strategically sensitive.

## Does reputation matter to a business?

For the technology industry, it increasingly does.

Public trust shapes regulation. Lawmakers find it easier to pass rules on companies that voters have come to distrust, and the United States is about to have a Congress controlled by the party more inclined to hold hearings on the industry. Democrats have already said they want to examine technology exports and the ties between prominent executives and the administration.

Trust also affects adoption. Businesses and governments choosing where to put their data, and individuals deciding how far to rely on an AI system, are influenced by whether they believe the companies behind the products are competent and careful.

None of that changes what is on a balance sheet this quarter. Nvidia's products are the same as they were a month ago, as we noted in our look at [what Huang's exit means for the AI industry](/tech/jensen-huang-exit-ai-industry-impact/). But reputations are slow to build and quick to become a punchline, and the industry has just handed its critics a week of material.

## The view from India

India's technology sector has mostly been spared this kind of treatment. Its large IT services firms are known for caution, and its public digital systems such as UPI enjoy broad trust.

That trust is an asset worth protecting. The lesson from America's bad week is that it depends on things users rarely see: systems that keep working, leaders who are careful, and prompt, straight answers when something does go wrong.
