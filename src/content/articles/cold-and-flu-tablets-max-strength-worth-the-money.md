When you have a blocked nose, a sore throat and a pounding head, a box marked "max strength" can look like the obvious choice. It is usually the expensive one.

In the UK, a pack of 16 branded all-in-one max-strength tablets sells for £7.30, more than 20 times the price of a basic pack of paracetamol in some supermarkets, the BBC found. Both contain the same 500mg of paracetamol.

"In reality, there's very little difference," Professor Jayne Lawrence, head of pharmacy at the University of Manchester, told the BBC. "Personally, I'd always go for the cheaper ones."

## Key points

- Most of the relief from cold and flu tablets comes from the painkiller, usually paracetamol.
- The decongestant in many tablets, phenylephrine, has little evidence of working when swallowed.
- Caffeine may make paracetamol work slightly faster, but the effect is modest.
- None of these medicines treats the infection. They relieve symptoms while it runs its course.

## What is in the box

| Ingredient | What it is meant to do | What the evidence says |
|---|---|---|
| Paracetamol | Relieves pain and lowers fever | Works. The same dose costs far less as a plain tablet |
| Ibuprofen | Relieves pain, fever and inflammation | Works. Not suitable for everyone |
| Phenylephrine | Clears a blocked nose | Little evidence it works as a tablet. The US Food and Drug Administration proposed in 2024 removing it from oral cold medicines as ineffective |
| Guaifenesin | Loosens mucus | Mixed evidence |
| Caffeine | Mild stimulant | May help paracetamol act slightly sooner |

Phenylephrine is largely broken down in the gut before it reaches the bloodstream, which is why it is thought to work poorly as a tablet but can work as a nasal spray.

Reckitt, which makes the Lemsip brand, told the BBC its ingredients are approved by regulators and formulated to treat several symptoms at once. The industry body for over-the-counter medicines says familiar brands help shoppers choose on crowded shelves.

## The real risk: doubling up on paracetamol

Combination products often contain paracetamol, and people take them alongside plain painkillers without realising. Too much paracetamol can cause serious liver damage, sometimes without early symptoms.

- **Read every label** and add up the paracetamol from all products.
- **Do not exceed the stated daily dose.** For most adults this is 4 grams a day, which is eight 500mg tablets, with at least four hours between doses. Lower limits apply to people with liver problems, low body weight or heavy alcohol use.
- **Check with a pharmacist** if you take other medicines, are pregnant, or have high blood pressure, heart disease or diabetes. Decongestants can raise blood pressure.

## What to buy instead

For most adults with a cold, a sensible kit costs very little:

- **Plain paracetamol or ibuprofen**, if suitable, for pain and fever.
- **A saline spray or steam** for a blocked nose. A decongestant nasal spray can help for a few days, but using it for more than about a week can make congestion worse.
- **Honey and warm drinks** for a sore throat and cough, except for babies under one year.
- **Rest and fluids.**

Hot powdered remedies cost more per dose. The warm drink may be soothing, but you can get the same effect with a hot lemon drink and a plain tablet.

## In India

The same pattern applies to Indian pharmacy shelves, where many cold tablets combine paracetamol with a decongestant and an antihistamine. India has banned a number of fixed-dose combination medicines in recent years on the ground that they lacked therapeutic justification. Checking the ingredients, rather than the brand, is the most reliable way to avoid paying for something you do not need, or taking a double dose of something you do.

**See a doctor** if you have difficulty breathing, chest pain, a high fever lasting more than three days, confusion, or symptoms that improve and then get worse. Antibiotics do not treat colds or flu, which are caused by viruses.
