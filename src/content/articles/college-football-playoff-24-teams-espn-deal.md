American college football is doubling the size of its championship tournament. The College Football Playoff will grow from 12 teams to 24 from the 2027 season, its organisers and the broadcaster ESPN announced on 9 November.

The change adds 11 games to the tournament and ends the conference championship games that have closed the regular season for about three decades. ESPN described the reworked contract as five years and an additional $1.7 billion, the Associated Press reported.

## Key points

- The management committee approved the expansion unanimously.
- Five places go automatically to champions. A selection committee chooses the other 19.
- ESPN's extra payment comes on top of an existing contract worth $7.8 billion that runs to the 2031 season.
- The first 24-team final is scheduled for 24 January 2028 in New Orleans.

## How the new format works

| Feature | From 2027 |
|---|---|
| Teams | 24, up from 12 |
| Automatic places | Champions of the four largest conferences, plus the best team from the six smaller ones |
| Other places | 19, chosen by a selection committee |
| First-round byes | The top eight seeds |
| Start | Second weekend of December |
| Venues | First two rounds at university stadiums, later rounds at traditional bowl games |
| Conference title games | Abolished |

Teams from the four largest conferences must play at least 10 qualifying games in the regular season to be eligible.

The playoff began in 2014 with four teams and moved to 12 in 2024. This is its second expansion in three years.

## The money

College football is among the most valuable properties in American television. Teams are attached to universities and the players were unpaid until recently, but the stadiums hold up to 100,000 people and the audiences rival those of professional leagues.

The arithmetic of the deal has two sides.

**What is gained.** Eleven more playoff games, each a knockout match with a national audience. ESPN is paying $1.7 billion more over five years for them.

**What is lost.** The title games of the four big conferences were estimated to be worth $345 million. According to a network report cited by the AP, ESPN will not have to pay about $140 million of the new money because those games no longer exist.

The colleges have not announced the terms themselves.

## Why broadcasters want more playoff games

Live sport is one of the few kinds of programming that people still watch at the moment it is shown, with the advertisements. As audiences for scheduled television shrink, the price of those hours has risen.

Knockout games are worth more than regular-season games because something is decided in each one. A larger tournament creates more of them, and keeps more teams' supporters interested late into the season.

Tony Petitti, commissioner of the Big Ten conference, has argued that a bigger playoff will bring more viewers to the regular season. Mark Keenum, who chairs the playoff's board, said the aim was to open it to "more programs, more student-athletes and more fan bases".

## The objections

- **Devaluing the season.** When about a third of the leading teams qualify, a single defeat matters less. Critics say famous late-season games will lose their stakes.
- **Trust in the selectors.** Nineteen places now depend on a committee whose past decisions have been disputed.
- **Smaller conferences.** Their share of places has shrunk with each expansion.
- **The players' workload.** A team that reaches the final could play 16 or 17 games.

## The same pattern elsewhere

Readers in India will recognise the logic. The Indian Premier League has expanded from eight teams to ten and lengthened its season as the value of its media rights has climbed, and football's World Cup and Champions League have both grown. In each case the organisers concluded that more matches of consequence were worth more than scarcity.

The risk is the same too. Every added game is sold on the promise that it matters, and there is a point at which audiences stop believing it.
