Advertisements for cosmetic surgery in Turkey are easy to find. They promise experienced surgeons, modern hospitals and prices far below those in Britain, western Europe or the United States, often with a hotel and airport transfers included.

Much of that can be true. Turkey has good hospitals and skilled surgeons, and many patients are satisfied. It is also true that some patients have died or returned home with serious complications, and that governments have issued warnings.

Promotional articles rarely mention the second part. This guide sets out what the official advice says and what to check before paying a deposit.

## Key points

- The UK Foreign Office says it is aware of seven British nationals who died in Turkey in 2025 after medical procedures.
- An earlier count from Foreign Office data found at least 28 such deaths between 2019 and March 2024.
- Official advice is to discuss plans with your own doctor first and to check a surgeon's qualifications independently.
- Flying too soon after surgery raises the risk of blood clots. Health guidance advises waiting several days.

## Why people travel

The main reason is price. A procedure can cost a fraction of what it does at home, largely because wages, rents and insurance are cheaper. Waiting times are short, and clinics sell complete packages.

Turkey has built a large industry on this. Medical tourism brings the country billions of dollars a year, and its government promotes it.

## What the official warnings say

Britain's Foreign Office updated its travel advice for Turkey in March 2026, stating that it was aware of seven British nationals who had died there in 2025 following medical procedures. It noted that others had suffered complications needing further treatment or surgery.

Its advice is to discuss any plan with a doctor, dentist or clinician at home before going ahead.

The British government has also run a public campaign on the risks. "We urge anyone considering a medical procedure abroad to review our travel advice, relevant guidance from the NHS and other professional bodies, and research foreign providers thoroughly," Foreign Office minister Stephen Doughty said.

Britain's professional bodies for plastic surgeons and dentists both advise against travelling for surgery without checking the qualifications of the person who will operate.

The numbers need context. Many thousands of people travel to Turkey for treatment each year, so deaths are rare. But they are not random. They cluster around certain procedures, rushed timetables and poor aftercare.

## Where the risks come from

**Too much surgery at once.** Packages that combine several operations in one session lengthen the time under anaesthetic and raise the risk of complications.

**Not enough time.** Some patients meet their surgeon for the first time on the day of the operation. A proper assessment, including medical history and tests, needs more than that.

**Flying home too soon.** Sitting on a long flight after surgery increases the risk of blood clots in the legs and lungs. NHS guidance suggests waiting five to seven days after procedures such as breast surgery and liposuction, and seven to ten days after facial surgery or a tummy tuck.

**Aftercare a continent away.** Infections and wound problems often appear days or weeks later, when the patient is home and the surgeon is not.

**Agents and salespeople.** Many bookings are made through intermediaries who are paid per patient and are not medically qualified.

**Infections that are hard to treat.** Health authorities in several countries have reported patients returning from surgery abroad with antibiotic-resistant infections.

## A checklist before you book

**About the surgeon**
1. What is the surgeon's full name, and where are they registered? Check the registration yourself.
2. Are they a specialist in plastic surgery, and how often do they perform this exact procedure?
3. Will you have a consultation with the surgeon, not a sales agent, before you travel?

**About the hospital**
4. Is it a licensed hospital with an intensive care unit, or a clinic that would have to transfer you in an emergency?
5. Is it approved by Turkey's Ministry of Health for international patients? Turkish authorities advise using approved providers.
6. Who gives the anaesthetic, and are they a qualified anaesthetist?

**About the plan**
7. Is only one major procedure planned per session?
8. How many days will you stay before flying, and does that match medical guidance?
9. What happens if something goes wrong: who pays for extra nights, further surgery or a flight home with medical support?

**About the paperwork**
10. Will you receive a written treatment plan, consent form and medical records in a language you read?
11. What does the price include, and what does it exclude?
12. Does your travel insurance cover planned surgery abroad? Most ordinary policies do not.

**About home**
13. Has your own doctor agreed the plan is suitable for you?
14. Who will treat you if a complication appears after you return?

If a clinic is unwilling to answer these, that is itself an answer.

## The true cost

A low price is only the first figure. Add flights, extra nights if recovery is slow, specialist insurance, time off work and the possible cost of corrective treatment at home.

Complications treated privately can cost more than the original saving. In countries with public healthcare, emergency treatment is given, but corrective cosmetic work generally is not.

## What Indian readers should know

**The same rules apply in every direction.** Indians travel abroad for some procedures, and India is itself one of the world's largest destinations for medical tourists. The checks above apply to both.

**In India, look for accreditation.** The National Accreditation Board for Hospitals and Healthcare Providers (NABH) certifies hospitals. A surgeon's registration can be checked with the relevant state medical council or the National Medical Commission.

**Insurance rarely covers cosmetic surgery.** Health policies in India generally exclude procedures done for appearance, covering reconstruction only when it is medically necessary, such as after an accident or cancer. Complications from an excluded procedure may also be excluded. Read the policy before assuming you are covered.

**Beware of offers on social media.** Many are placed by agents. Deal with the hospital directly and verify every claim independently.

Cosmetic surgery is real surgery, wherever it is done. A cheaper price can be good value. It is not a reason to skip the checks.
