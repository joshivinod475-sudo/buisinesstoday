Vegetarian and Vegan Dining in Covent Garden: How to Find a Good Meal Before the Show

Covent Garden has many plant-based options, but vegan and vegetarian labels can hide butter, ghee, cheese or honey. Here is how to choose a restaurant, check a menu and book pre-theatre.

Editorial Desk Published 24 Nov 2026, 11:32 pm IST 4 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

Covent Garden, in London’s West End, is packed with restaurants serving theatre-goers, shoppers and tourists. Finding food that is vegetarian is easy. Finding a meal that is fully vegan, and not just a side salad, takes a little more planning.

Here is how to check a menu properly, which cuisines tend to suit plant-based diners, and how to time a meal before a show.

Key points

  • Vegetarian food excludes meat and fish. Vegan food also excludes dairy, eggs, honey and other animal products.
  • A “vegetarian” dish may still contain cheese made with animal rennet, butter, ghee, cream or egg.
  • Indian, Middle Eastern, Ethiopian, East Asian and Italian kitchens often have the widest naturally plant-based choices.
  • UK restaurants must tell customers if a dish contains any of 14 regulated allergens, including milk and eggs. Use this to check vegan claims.
  • Book ahead for pre-theatre slots, usually from about 5pm to 6:30pm.

Vegetarian is not vegan

Menus in London increasingly label dishes as “V” (vegetarian) and “VE” or “PB” (vegan or plant-based). The difference matters:

Ingredient Vegetarian? Vegan?
Milk, cream, butter, yoghurt Yes No
Ghee (clarified butter) Yes No
Paneer Yes, if made without animal rennet No
Hard cheese such as Parmesan Often no, as it is traditionally made with animal rennet No
Eggs Yes, for most vegetarians No
Honey Yes No, for most vegans
Fish sauce, anchovies, gelatine No No

Watch for less obvious sources: butter brushed on naan or pastry, egg washes on bread, fish sauce in South-East Asian dishes and gelatine in desserts.

Why Indian food suits plant-based diners

India has one of the world’s oldest vegetarian traditions, shaped by religion, including Jain, Hindu and Buddhist practice, and by regional farming. Much of Indian cooking is built on lentils, chickpeas, beans, vegetables, rice and wheat, so a large part of a typical menu is already vegetarian.

For vegans, the main things to check are ghee, cream, butter, yoghurt and paneer. Ask:

  • Is the dal finished with butter or ghee, or cooked in oil?
  • Is the naan made with yoghurt or milk, or brushed with butter? Roti and chapati are often simpler.
  • Is the sauce thickened with cream, or with cashews, coconut or tomato?
  • Can the kitchen cook a dish in oil instead of ghee?

Dishes such as chana masala (chickpeas), aloo gobi (potato and cauliflower), bhindi (okra), baingan (aubergine), many dals and vegetable biryani can often be made fully vegan.

Jain food is a useful clue for some diners. Jain diets are vegetarian and usually exclude root vegetables such as onion, garlic and potato. Jain dishes are not automatically vegan, since dairy is often used, but restaurants that offer them are usually used to detailed questions about ingredients.

How to check a menu

  1. Look for a separate vegan menu, not just symbols on the main menu. A dedicated menu usually means the kitchen has thought about full meals.
  2. Read the allergen information. Under UK law, restaurants must tell you if a dish contains milk, eggs, fish or crustaceans, among the 14 regulated allergens. The Food Standards Agency’s guidance on allergen information says written information should be available, backed up by staff.
  3. Ask about shared fryers and cross-contact if this matters to you, for example if you have a milk allergy rather than a dietary choice.
  4. Mention your needs when booking, so the kitchen can prepare.
  5. Check recent reviews and the current menu online. Restaurants in the area change menus and owners often.

Planning around a show

Covent Garden is the heart of London’s theatre district. Restaurants fill quickly between about 5pm and 7pm, when audiences eat before evening performances, and again before weekend matinees.

  • Book a pre-theatre table and say what time your show starts. Many restaurants offer set pre-theatre menus, so check whether vegan options are included.
  • Allow time to walk. Most West End theatres are within 15 minutes on foot of the Piazza, but crowds slow you down.
  • Eat after the show if you cannot get an early table. Some restaurants serve late.

Many diners who avoid animal products also avoid gluten. Indian, Thai and Ethiopian cuisines rely heavily on rice, lentils and gram flour, but check for wheat in breads, batters and thickened sauces.

For Indian visitors in London

Indian vegetarian travellers often find London easier than many European cities. Besides restaurants in central London, areas such as Wembley, Southall and Tooting have large numbers of vegetarian Indian restaurants and sweet shops. If you are touring London’s curry restaurants, our guide to checking spice levels before ordering on Brick Lane explains how British curry house menus work.

Vegetarians and vegans should also make sure they get enough iron and other nutrients. Our explainer on how to absorb more iron without meat or supplements covers the basics.

Frequently asked questions

Is paneer vegan? No. Paneer is a cheese made from milk. It is vegetarian if made without animal rennet.

Is naan vegan? Often not. Many naan recipes use yoghurt or milk and are brushed with butter. Ask, or choose roti.

What does “plant-based” mean on a menu? Usually that the dish contains no animal products, but it is worth confirming with staff.

When should I book a pre-theatre dinner in Covent Garden? As early as possible, especially on Fridays and Saturdays.
