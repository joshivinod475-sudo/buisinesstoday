Newsom Visits Ukraine and Pledges California Medical Aid as Trump Prepares to Meet Putin

California's governor made an unannounced trip to Lviv and promised hospital beds, generators and a field medical station. Here is what was pledged and why it matters.

Editorial Desk Published 17 Nov 2026, 9:44 am IST 4 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

Gavin Newsom, the governor of California, made an unannounced visit to Ukraine on Sunday and said his state would send a new shipment of medical aid. His office disclosed the trip only after he had left the country.

The visit to the western city of Lviv came days before President Donald Trump is due to meet Vladimir Putin in China, and as Newsom considers running for president in 2028.

Key points

  • California will donate more than 1,300 hospital beds, 20 portable generators, more than 11 million bandages and a 50-bed deployable medical station.
  • The aid is being sent through a humanitarian organisation. No cash value has been given.
  • Newsom met the governor of the Lviv region and visited a military cemetery, a rehabilitation hospital and defence technology companies.
  • A US state cannot conduct foreign policy, but it can give aid and sign trade and cultural agreements.

What California is sending

According to the governor’s office, the donation from the state’s emergency services agency includes:

Item Quantity
Deployable medical station, with oxygen concentrators and wheelchairs One, with 50 beds
Hospital beds More than 1,300
Bandages More than 11 million
Wound care kits Thousands
Portable generators 20

California has sent medical supplies to Ukraine since 2022, including five field medical stations in the first months of the war, largely through the California-based charity Direct Relief.

“No people fighting for their freedom should have to wonder whether they stand alone,” Newsom said in a statement. “California stands with Ukraine.”

What he did in Lviv

Newsom met Maksym Kozytskyy, governor of the Lviv region, to discuss cooperation, including on energy security. California’s legislature established a sister-state relationship with the region in 2024, and the two signed a memorandum on trade and reconstruction earlier this year.

He visited the Field of Honour at Lychakiv Cemetery, where more than 1,000 Ukrainian soldiers have been buried since 2022, and a rehabilitation centre that fits prosthetic limbs for people wounded in the war. He also toured a group of defence technology firms that make drones and ground robots.

He had come from Poland, where he met Ukrainian and American energy executives, and from the United Nations climate conference in Turkey.

What a governor can and cannot do

Under the US constitution, foreign policy belongs to the federal government. A state cannot sign treaties, send weapons or impose its own sanctions regime.

States can do other things. They can donate surplus equipment, sign non-binding agreements on trade, research and culture, and direct their own agencies and pension funds. California’s National Guard has had a training partnership with Ukraine’s armed forces since 1993, under a federal programme.

California’s economy is larger than that of most countries, which gives its governor a hearing abroad that other state leaders do not get.

The political background

The trip puts Newsom in visible contrast with the president. Trump is pressing for a negotiated end to the war and has had a tense relationship with Ukraine’s president, Volodymyr Zelenskyy. He eased sanctions on Russian diesel exports in October, and is due to meet Putin and China’s Xi Jinping in Shenzhen on Thursday, as we report in our article on Trump’s trip to Asia.

In Poland, Newsom described California as “a stable partner regardless of the whiplash in Washington”.

His term as governor ends in January. He has said he will decide early in the new year whether to seek the Democratic nomination in 2028, and foreign trips of this kind help a candidate build a record on international affairs.

Why it matters for business

Reconstruction. Rebuilding Ukraine is expected to be one of the largest construction and infrastructure efforts in Europe for decades. Agreements between regions, like the one between California and Lviv, are how companies position themselves for that work.

Defence technology. Ukraine has become a centre for low-cost drones and battlefield software, and Western companies and investors are seeking partnerships there. Europe’s effort to build its own capacity is the subject of our report on alternatives to Palantir.

Energy. Attacks on Ukraine’s power system have made generators and grid equipment a constant need, which explains their place in the donation.

India’s position. India has kept ties with both Russia and Ukraine, buying discounted Russian oil while sending humanitarian aid to Kyiv. Any settlement, or any change in sanctions, affects the price India pays for energy. Tension on Europe’s eastern edge is also rising, as we reported in our article on Estonia’s plan to close its border with Russia.

This article draws on a statement from the office of the Governor of California and on news agency reporting published by The Spokesman-Review. The value of the donated equipment has not been published.
