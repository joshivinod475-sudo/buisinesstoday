Phil Vickery, CTE and Rugby: What the Brain Disease Is and Why 1,100 Players Are Suing

England World Cup winner Phil Vickery has spoken about a probable diagnosis of the brain disease CTE. Here is what CTE is, how it is diagnosed and where the legal case stands.

Editorial Desk Published 17 Nov 2026, 9:40 am IST 4 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

Phil Vickery, who won the Rugby World Cup with England in 2003 and later captained the side, has spoken publicly about living with a probable diagnosis of chronic traumatic encephalopathy, a brain disease linked to repeated blows to the head.

Vickery, 50, told CNN that a brain scan two years ago showed damage. He is one of more than 1,100 former players taking legal action against the sport’s governing bodies, which deny liability.

His account is a useful starting point for understanding a condition that affects many contact sports and is still not fully understood.

Key points

  • CTE is a progressive brain disease associated with repeated head impacts.
  • It can be confirmed only by examining the brain after death. In a living person, doctors can say only that it is probable.
  • Symptoms can include memory loss, depression, anxiety and difficulty controlling impulses.
  • There is no cure. Symptoms can be treated.

What Vickery has described

Vickery told CNN his symptoms became noticeable six or seven years ago. He struggled to recall names and places, including those of people close to him, and found himself becoming angry. He also has tinnitus, a constant ringing in the ears.

He said he wore earplugs at a recent England match to cope with the noise and emotion of the occasion.

After retiring, he said, he spent years avoiding the fact that his playing career was over. A former coach then challenged him to enter a bodybuilding competition. He followed an 18-week programme of training and strict diet, and competed. He has said he hopes that talking about it helps others who are struggling.

He has also said he holds no bitterness and would play rugby again.

What CTE is

CTE stands for chronic traumatic encephalopathy. It is a degenerative disease in which an abnormal form of a protein called tau builds up in the brain and damages nerve cells.

It is associated with repeated head impacts over years. These include concussions, and also the more frequent smaller knocks that cause no obvious symptoms at the time. It was first described in boxers in the 1920s and has since been found in the brains of people who played American football, rugby, football and ice hockey, and in military veterans.

Two points are often misunderstood.

  • It is not the same as concussion. A concussion is a single injury from which most people recover. CTE develops over a long period.
  • Not everyone exposed develops it. Many people who play contact sports for years do not. Why some do and others do not is not yet known.

Why it cannot be confirmed in life

The tau deposits that define CTE can be seen only under a microscope, in brain tissue examined after death. Scans of a living brain can show damage or shrinkage, but cannot yet prove the cause.

That is why doctors use the word “probable”, and why Vickery and other former players have pledged to donate their brains to research.

The symptoms linked to CTE also overlap with those of other conditions, including depression, other forms of dementia and the effects of alcohol. Anyone with these symptoms needs a proper medical assessment, because some of the other causes are treatable.

The legal case

More than 1,100 former rugby union and rugby league players have brought a claim in England against World Rugby, the Rugby Football Union, the Welsh Rugby Union, the Rugby Football League and others.

They allege the bodies failed in their duty to protect players from the risk of brain injury. The claimants report conditions including probable CTE, early-onset dementia, Parkinson’s disease and motor neurone disease.

All the governing bodies have denied liability. The case has not been decided.

Vickery told CNN that a financial award would not change his diagnosis. He said he wants accountability and for players’ accounts to be heard in court.

What sport has changed

Rugby’s authorities have introduced measures over the past decade, including formal head injury assessments during matches, mandatory rest periods after a concussion, lower legal tackle heights in the amateur game and limits on contact in training. Trials of mouthguards fitted with sensors now measure the force of impacts.

Critics, including some of the claimants, say the changes came late and do not go far enough.

If you play or coach a contact sport

This applies as much to kabaddi, football, hockey, boxing and wrestling in India as to rugby, and to cricket, where concussion substitutes were introduced in 2019.

  1. If in doubt, sit them out. A player with a suspected concussion should leave the field and not return that day.
  2. Know the signs. These include confusion, headache, dizziness, nausea, blurred vision and memory gaps. A person does not need to have been knocked out.
  3. Return gradually. Rest, then light activity, then training, with medical clearance before any contact.
  4. Take extra care with children. Young brains take longer to recover.
  5. Reduce head contact in training. Most impacts happen in practice, where they can be limited.

Seek urgent medical help after a head injury if there is repeated vomiting, a worsening headache, drowsiness, a seizure, weakness in a limb or unusual behaviour.

Low mood and thoughts of self-harm can follow brain injury and can accompany these conditions. Our guide to mental health warning signs and how to get help lists where to turn. For staying active in later life without contact sport, see our article on how to stay fit as you age.

This article is general information and not medical advice. Anyone worried about memory, mood or the effects of a head injury should see a doctor. It draws on Phil Vickery’s interview with CNN and on published medical descriptions of CTE. The legal claims described are allegations that the defendants deny.
