The US Has Reportedly Lost Its Measles Elimination Status After 26 Years. What That Means

Health authorities have concluded that measles is spreading continuously in the US again, reports say, with more than 5,400 cases in 2026. Here is what the status means.

Editorial Desk Published 15 Nov 2026, 10:41 pm IST 5 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

The United States has lost its status as a country that has eliminated measles, according to reports by NPR, CNN and KFF Health News. It had held that status since 2000.

The decision was made by experts advising the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), the regional arm of the World Health Organization, the reports say. PAHO is expected to announce it formally at a news conference on Tuesday, 17 November. Until then the finding rests on reporting that cites people with knowledge of the decision.

The numbers behind it are not in dispute. Here is what the status means and why it has been lost.

Key points

  • The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) had counted 5,412 confirmed measles cases in 2026 as of 12 November, more than double last year’s total.
  • Cases have been confirmed in 45 states and Washington, DC, with more than 590 people admitted to hospital.
  • A country loses elimination status when the same chain of measles infection continues for 12 months or more.
  • About 96% of this year’s US cases were in people who were unvaccinated or whose vaccination status was unknown, the CDC says.

What “elimination” means

Elimination does not mean a disease has disappeared. It means the virus is no longer spreading continuously inside a country. Cases still occur when travellers bring the infection in, but each outbreak is contained and dies out.

The test is specific. A country is considered to have eliminated measles when there has been no continuous local transmission of the same strain for at least 12 months, and it has a surveillance system good enough to prove it. If one chain of infection keeps going for a year, the status is lost.

According to the reports, PAHO’s experts reviewed the CDC’s case and laboratory data and found that one strain had been spreading continuously for 12 months or longer.

The US had come close before. Outbreaks linked to Disneyland in 2015 and in New York in 2018 and 2019 were contained in time. This one was not.

How large the outbreak is

  • Cases: 5,412 confirmed by 12 November, the CDC reported.
  • Spread: 45 states and Washington, DC. Pennsylvania has the largest outbreak, with more than 1,500 cases.
  • Outbreaks: 43 new outbreaks this year, defined as three or more linked cases. About a third of this year’s cases belong to outbreaks that began in 2025 and never stopped.
  • Hospital admissions: more than 590.
  • Deaths: counts differ. The CDC has confirmed four deaths this year. State health departments have reported more, and CBS News puts the figure at six or more.

Measles is among the most contagious diseases known. The virus can stay in the air of a room for up to two hours after an infected person has left. Complications include pneumonia, swelling of the brain, deafness and death, and they are most common in young children.

Why it happened

The answer in the data is vaccination.

Two doses of the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine are about 97% effective at preventing measles. To stop the virus spreading, PAHO recommends that at least 95% of a population be vaccinated. Childhood vaccination rates have fallen below that level in many American communities.

Of this year’s cases, 96% were in people who were unvaccinated or whose status was unknown, and only 2% were fully vaccinated, the CDC says.

Rules on school vaccination are set by each US state. Nine states made it easier to opt out in 2025, CBS News reported. States that ended non-medical exemptions saw vaccination among children starting school rise by two to four percentage points.

Infants, pregnant women and people with weak immune systems cannot always be vaccinated and depend on the protection of those around them. Dr Omer Awan, a physician quoted by CBS News, warned that they will bear the worst of the consequences. One such case is the death of an infant in Wisconsin reported this month.

What changes now

In legal or trade terms, nothing. Elimination status carries no sanctions and no travel restrictions.

The practical effects are these:

  • Doctors must think of measles first. In a country without local spread, a fever and rash in someone who has not travelled is unlikely to be measles. That assumption no longer holds.
  • The US may export cases. Travellers can now carry measles from the US to countries that have kept it out.
  • Costs rise. Each outbreak requires contact tracing, testing, quarantine and hospital care, paid for by health departments and insurers.

The US is not alone. Canada lost its own elimination status in November 2025 after a year of continuous spread.

How a country gets the status back

The route is simple to state and hard to do. Transmission of the virus must stop, and the country must then show at least 12 months without continuous spread, backed by surveillance data that PAHO verifies. In practice that means raising vaccination rates, finding cases quickly and responding to each outbreak fast.

What Indian readers should know

India is still working towards elimination. Measles and rubella vaccination is part of the government’s Universal Immunisation Programme, with two doses given free: the first at 9 to 12 months and the second at 16 to 24 months. The same 95% coverage target applies.

Check before you travel. Anyone travelling to the US, or anywhere with an active outbreak, should confirm that they and their children have had two doses. Adults who are unsure can ask a doctor about a blood test or a booster.

It is a business story as well. Outbreaks close schools, keep parents off work and load costs on to hospitals and insurers. Prevention through routine vaccination costs a small fraction of what an outbreak does.
