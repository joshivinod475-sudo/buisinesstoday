Trying to Get Pregnant: What Helps, What Does Not, and When to See a Doctor

Most couples conceive within a year. Here is what health services advise on timing, folic acid, weight, smoking and alcohol, for women and men, and when to get help.

Editorial Desk Published 16 Nov 2026, 9:27 am IST 5 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

Couples who decide to have a baby often expect it to happen within a month or two. When it does not, worry sets in quickly, and so does a flood of advice from relatives, websites and people selling supplements.

The reassuring fact is that it usually takes longer than people expect, and that this is normal. Britain’s National Health Service (NHS) says that more than 8 in 10 couples in which the woman is under 40 will conceive naturally within a year if they have regular unprotected sex.

This guide sets out what established health services advise, what makes a real difference, and when it is time to see a doctor. It is general information, and a doctor who knows your history should guide any decision.

Key points

  • Having sex every two to three days throughout the month is the standard advice. There is no need to time it precisely.
  • Folic acid is recommended for women before conception and in early pregnancy.
  • Smoking, alcohol and being significantly over or underweight all reduce fertility, in men as well as women.
  • See a doctor after a year of trying, or sooner if the woman is 36 or over or there is a known health problem.

How long it normally takes

According to the NHS:

  • more than 8 in 10 couples, where the woman is under 40, conceive within a year of regular unprotected sex
  • around 1 in 7 couples may have difficulty conceiving
  • fertility declines with age, in both women and men

A few months without success is therefore expected, not a sign that something is wrong.

Timing: simpler than the apps suggest

A woman is most likely to conceive around the time she ovulates, which usually happens about 14 days before her next period begins. In a 28-day cycle that is around day 14. In a longer or shorter cycle it shifts.

The NHS advice does not ask couples to pinpoint the day. It says that having sex every two to three days without contraception is enough to cover the fertile time. Ovulation kits and tracking apps can help some people. They can also add pressure, and stress helps nobody.

What women can do

Take folic acid. Health services recommend a daily folic acid supplement while trying to conceive and through the first three months of pregnancy, because it lowers the risk of serious defects of the baby’s brain and spine. The usual dose is 400 micrograms. Some women need a higher dose prescribed by a doctor, including those with diabetes or epilepsy or with a previous affected pregnancy.

Reach a healthy weight. The NHS says being overweight or severely underweight can affect ovulation.

Stop smoking and avoid alcohol. Both reduce fertility and both harm a developing baby. The NHS advises avoiding alcohol altogether while trying to conceive.

Review your medicines. Tell your doctor you are trying to conceive. Do not stop a prescribed medicine without advice.

Check your vaccinations and screening. The NHS suggests confirming that you are protected against rubella, through the MMR vaccine, and that cervical screening is up to date. Rubella in pregnancy can cause serious harm to the baby.

Get existing conditions under control. Diabetes, thyroid disease, high blood pressure and epilepsy are all best managed before pregnancy.

What men can do

Fertility is not only a woman’s matter. A problem on the man’s side is involved in a large share of couples who have difficulty.

  • Stop smoking. The NHS says smoking, including breathing in other people’s smoke, can reduce semen quality.
  • Limit alcohol.
  • Keep to a healthy weight.
  • Avoid anabolic steroids and recreational drugs, which can sharply reduce sperm production.
  • Be aware of workplace exposure. Certain pesticides, solvents and metals can affect fertility, particularly in men.
  • Get tested early. A semen analysis is simple and inexpensive, and should be one of the first investigations, not the last.

What does not help

  • Expensive “fertility” supplements. Beyond folic acid for women, there is little good evidence for most products sold to boost fertility. Some contain doses that are not advisable in pregnancy.
  • Special diets that promise conception. A balanced diet matters. No single food produces a pregnancy.
  • Blaming stress. Severe stress can have an effect, but ordinary worry is not the reason most couples take time to conceive. Being told to “just relax” is not a treatment.
  • Unproven remedies and clinics that guarantee results. No honest practitioner can guarantee a pregnancy.

When to see a doctor

The NHS advises seeing a doctor if you have not conceived after a year of trying.

Go sooner if:

  • the woman is aged 36 or over
  • periods are irregular, very painful or absent
  • either partner has a known condition, such as a previous pelvic infection, endometriosis, a sexually transmitted infection, or past cancer treatment
  • there have been repeated miscarriages
  • sex is difficult or painful

Both partners should be assessed together. Tests usually begin with blood tests and an ultrasound for the woman and a semen analysis for the man.

What treatment involves

Depending on the cause, the NHS lists three broad approaches:

  • Medicines to help with ovulation
  • Surgery, for example to treat endometriosis or repair blocked tubes
  • Assisted conception, such as intrauterine insemination (IUI) or in vitro fertilisation (IVF)

Many couples need only the first. IVF is not the automatic next step.

For couples in India

  • Fertility treatment is regulated. Clinics and donor banks must be registered under the Assisted Reproductive Technology (Regulation) Act, 2021. Ask to see a clinic’s registration before starting treatment.
  • Ask for costs in writing. An IVF cycle in a private clinic typically costs between one and several lakh rupees, and more than one cycle is often needed. Request an itemised estimate, including medicines and tests.
  • Check your health insurance. Many policies in India exclude infertility treatment, though some newer plans include limited cover. Read the policy wording.
  • Be wary of promised success rates. Ask how the clinic calculates its figures and what they are for someone of your age.
  • Resist the pressure. Family expectations can make a normal few months of trying feel like a crisis. Medical guidance, not anxious relatives, should set the timetable.

For those planning the finances, it helps to treat this like any other large expense: know the likely total, keep an emergency fund separate, and avoid high-interest borrowing where possible.

This article is general information and not medical advice. Fertility depends on individual circumstances, so speak to a qualified doctor about your own situation. It draws on guidance published by Britain’s National Health Service.
