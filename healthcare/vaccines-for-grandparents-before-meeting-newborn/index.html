A New Grandchild on the Way? The Vaccines Grandparents and Carers Should Check Before Meeting a Newborn

Newborns are too young for most vaccines, so the adults around them are their protection. Here are the vaccines doctors advise checking, and when to get them.

Editorial Desk Published 15 Nov 2026, 10:31 pm IST 5 min read
Share WhatsApp X LinkedIn

When a baby is due, the family prepares the cot, the clothes and the car seat. Doctors suggest adding one more item to the list, and it is for the adults: a check of their own vaccinations.

A newborn cannot be vaccinated against most infections for the first weeks or months of life. During that time, the baby’s protection depends on the people who hold it. For grandparents, who are often the most frequent visitors and helpers, that makes a visit to the doctor before the birth worth the trouble.

Key points

  • Babies cannot have the whooping cough vaccine until about two months old, or flu and Covid-19 vaccines until six months.
  • The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) advises everyone around a newborn to be up to date on whooping cough and flu vaccines.
  • Vaccines take about two weeks to give protection, so they should be had well before the first visit.
  • Good hygiene and staying away when ill matter as much as vaccines.

Why newborns depend on the adults around them

A baby’s immune system is still developing at birth, and the first vaccines only begin weeks later. In that gap, infections that are mild in an adult can be serious in an infant.

Adults are often the source. A grandparent with what feels like a lingering cough may in fact have whooping cough, and may pass it on without knowing.

Doctors at the Cedars-Sinai hospital in Los Angeles describe vaccinated family members as the baby’s first line of defence. Dr Jonathan Grein, who leads infection control there, says keeping vaccines up to date stops carers from unknowingly passing a serious illness to a baby.

The vaccines to check

Vaccine Why it matters for the baby Who should check
Whooping cough (Tdap) Can cause severe coughing fits, pneumonia and death in infants Any adult without a dose in the past 10 years
Flu Babies under six months are at high risk of complications and cannot be vaccinated Everyone, every year
Covid-19 Severe illness in infants is uncommon but does occur Adults, as currently recommended
RSV A leading cause of hospital admission in babies Older adults, as advised by a doctor
Measles Highly contagious and dangerous to infants Adults unsure of their immunity

Whooping cough. This is the one doctors stress most. The combined vaccine against tetanus, diphtheria and whooping cough is known as Tdap. The CDC recommends one dose for any adult who has never had it, with a booster every ten years. Pregnant women are advised to have it during each pregnancy, between weeks 27 and 36, so that protection passes to the baby before birth.

Flu. A yearly flu vaccine is recommended for everyone aged six months and over. Since babies below that age cannot have it, the vaccination of those around them is their only protection.

RSV. Respiratory syncytial virus causes a cold in adults and can cause serious breathing trouble in babies. In the US a vaccine is recommended for adults aged 75 and over and for some younger adults at higher risk. Babies can be protected directly, through a vaccine given to the mother late in pregnancy or an antibody injection given to the infant.

Measles. With measles spreading widely in the United States this year, doctors there are also advising grandparents to confirm they are immune. People born before 1957 are generally presumed to be immune because most had the disease as children. Others may need a dose.

When to get them

Vaccines do not work immediately. The body needs about two weeks to build protection.

  • Aim to complete vaccinations two to four weeks before the first meeting with the baby.
  • Several vaccines can usually be given at the same visit.
  • If the baby has already arrived, it is still worth doing. Protection begins about two weeks later.

Other ways to protect a newborn

Vaccines are one layer. The everyday measures matter just as much:

  • wash hands before holding the baby
  • stay away if you have a fever, cough, cold or upset stomach, however much you want to visit
  • avoid kissing a newborn on the face, especially if you have a cold sore
  • consider a mask if a respiratory illness is going around
  • do not smoke near the baby

“Newborns don’t need a sterile world. They need a prepared one,” says Dr Pamela Phillips, a paediatrician at Cedars-Sinai.

Having the conversation

Asking relatives about their vaccinations can feel awkward. Phillips suggests framing it around love and protection for the baby, and not as a demand. Many families find it easiest to share what the baby’s doctor has advised, so that the request comes from the doctor and not from the new parents.

For grandparents, getting it done before being asked removes the awkwardness altogether.

What this means for families in India

The principle is the same anywhere: protect the baby by protecting the people around it. The details differ.

  • During pregnancy. Tetanus and diphtheria vaccination for pregnant women is part of India’s government programme. Many obstetricians also recommend the version that covers whooping cough, and a flu vaccine. Ask the doctor at an antenatal visit.
  • For grandparents and other adults. Adult vaccination is not routine in India, so it has to be asked for. Flu and Tdap vaccines are available at private hospitals and clinics. A doctor can advise which are appropriate, taking into account age and existing conditions such as diabetes or heart disease.
  • For the baby. The first vaccines are given at birth and the schedule continues at 6, 10 and 14 weeks. Keeping to those dates is the most important protection of all.
  • In a joint family. Where many relatives share a home, it helps to agree simple rules for the first two months: clean hands, no visits from anyone who is unwell, and limited handling by visitors.

The stakes are clearest in the worst cases. This month an infant died of measles in Wisconsin, too young to have been fully vaccinated.

This article is general information and not medical advice. Vaccine recommendations depend on age, health and country, so speak to a doctor about what is right for you. It draws on guidance from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and from doctors at Cedars-Sinai.
